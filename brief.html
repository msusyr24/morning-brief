🎥 From People I Follow

Peter Attia

Lisa Feldman Barrett makes a compelling case that emotions are not discrete, hardwired biological categories (fear, anger, sadness) but are instead constructed predictions — the brain's best guess at what sensory signals mean given context. The key evidence: when you look at actual data, there's no reliable physiological or facial signature that uniquely identifies any emotion. Anger produces scowling only ~35% of the time; the amygdala activates during "fear" only ~30% of the time. Variance is the norm, not the exception. This has real implications for emotional regulation: if emotions aren't happening to you but are being actively built by your brain, you have more leverage over them than the classical model suggests. Attia also posted a notable self-correction on Zone 2 training: the 80/20 (zone 2 / high intensity) split was derived from high-volume endurance athletes, and he now believes it doesn't apply to people training under ~4 hours/week of cardio — at low volumes, higher-intensity work likely produces better returns.

Videos: The neuroscience of emotion — Lisa Feldman Barrett · Why Peter changed his mind on Zone 2 · How Peter's views have changed over 8 years

Jared Alderman (with Uri Peleg)

Jared opens with an interesting theoretical question: what if you randomized your bet sizing rather than just your frequency? Uri's response is characteristically sharp — practically speaking, you're already playing a one-size game tree that most players haven't fully mapped, so randomizing sizes adds complexity without proportional benefit. The more useful insight Uri draws out: your opponent doesn't know your game tree, which means you can systematically deviate from GTO frequencies across different board textures (over-bluff some, under-bluff others) as long as you're globally in the ballpark — there's no practical way for opponents to detect this. He illustrates with a heads-up example where he wasn't RNGing at all, and his opponent couldn't tell. The broader takeaway: hidden information is underutilized, and the gap between what you know about your own strategy and what your opponent can infer is a real, exploitable edge.

Renaissance Periodization (Mike Israetel)

Two sharp, concise clips. On GLP-1s (Ozempic/tirzepatide): without resistance training and high protein intake, weight loss on these drugs produces a "deflated" look rather than a body recomposition — muscle is lost alongside fat. The fix is simple: lift at least 2–4 days/week and prioritize protein, and the transformation becomes exceptional. On myostatin inhibitors: the current injectable versions cost an estimated $350K/year, making them impractical for anyone but elite athletes. The real milestone to watch for is an oral small-molecule myostatin inhibitor (none yet exist), which Israetel predicts will have the cultural uptake of Ozempic and provide a meaningful tool for preserving muscle during GLP-1-driven weight loss.

Thomas DeLauer (with Dr. John Sheff)

For people with severe metabolic dysfunction — fasting insulin >15, visceral fat index >10, evidence of fatty liver — Dr. Sheff argues standard intermittent fasting protocols (like 36-hour fasts) are the wrong tool. The liver is effectively "offline," meaning the system that governs gluconeogenesis, ketone production, and fat oxidation isn't working correctly. He observes patients fasting 2–3 days with fasting glucose that never drops below 100, because gluconeogenesis keeps running and cells remain glucose-dependent. His clinical protocol: 3-day fasts, repeated every 3 weeks, until fasting glucose normalizes — a more aggressive "reboot" designed to force hepatic insulin resistance to resolve before optimization-level tools like shorter fasts can take effect. The distinction he draws is between a mainframe that's down vs. one that just needs a software update.

Figs in Winter (Massimo Pigliucci)

Pigliucci profiles Gaius Blossius, a Stoic philosopher who was a close ally of Tiberius Gracchus and participated in his radical land reform movement — challenging the common perception that Stoicism is inherently quietist or politically passive. The piece raises the genuine philosophical question of when Stoic principles demand action rather than acceptance, and how the dichotomy of control doesn't necessarily counsel withdrawal from political life. A good counterweight to the "just meditate and endure" readings of the tradition.

Lisa Feldman Barrett: Emotions Are Constructed, Not Hardwired — and That Changes How You Can Regulate Them

The specific implication worth sitting with from the Attia interview: if emotions are predictions your brain constructs (not readouts of a hardwired circuit), then the Stoic project of modifying your judgments about events is mechanistically supported by modern neuroscience. Barrett's data shows that physiological and behavioral variance within a single emotion category is so high that there's no reliable "fear response" or "anger response" — context and appraisal shape the construction. The practical upshot: you can interrupt the construction process earlier than classical emotion theory would suggest, because the emotion isn't already there waiting to be triggered — it's being actively built.

Peter Attia Podcast #413

Zone 2 Recalibration: The 80/20 Rule Only Applies at High Training Volumes

Attia's self-correction is worth internalizing: the 80% Zone 2 / 20% high-intensity split comes from elite endurance athlete research where total weekly volume is very high, and Zone 2 is used to accumulate work without exceeding recovery capacity. At under 4 hours/week of cardio — which describes most people — this logic inverts. Low-volume trainees should shift toward higher-intensity work because they're not at risk of overreaching. The exception: complete beginners, for whom any movement at any intensity is the right first step.
