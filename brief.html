🎥 From People I Follow

Worth your time:

Jared Alderman

Alderman makes a sharp point about the psychology of passive lines: players conflate aggression with skill and passivity with weakness, when in poker both checking and betting are equally legitimate decisions. The real error is betting small as "insurance" — paying a little to "end the hand now" — which is actually a sign of low confidence, not control. He argues that vs. recreational players, you want to build a more nuanced two-dimensional map of their tendencies (e.g., someone who check-raises top pair but never bluffs) rather than flattening them into labels like "station" or "aggro," because that nuance is where actual exploitation lives.

Thomas DeLauer

DeLauer covers a 12-week study (Journal of Medicinal Food) on obese men with metabolic syndrome and confirmed fatty liver — BMI 36+, high fasting glucose. The diet was a Spanish low-carb Mediterranean protocol heavy on olive oil, poultry, fish, eggs, and full-fat dairy. Results: triglycerides cut in half (232→111), HDL up from 42→58, fasting glucose 118→90, ALT liver enzyme 71→37, and 100% of subjects were free of metabolic syndrome by week 12 — even though all were still technically overweight. DeLauer argues the olive oil did double duty: ketogenic fat plus anti-inflammatory polyphenols, which is why results outpaced weight loss. He also introduces the protein leverage hypothesis — the idea that humans keep eating until protein needs are satisfied, so front-loading protein early in the day naturally suppresses total caloric intake without deliberate restriction.

Dr. Sten Ekberg

Ekberg challenges the foundational claim of Robert Lustig's famous 25M-view sugar lecture: that fructose is processed exclusively by the liver (making it analogous to alcohol). A 2018 discovery showed the small intestine actually processes fructose first — clearing ~90% of it at low doses before any reaches the liver. The liver is the backup, not the first stop. The critical insight is that this mechanism is dose-dependent: the gut saturates quickly (roughly 10–25g of fructose per sitting in humans, depending on absorption speed), and anything above that threshold overflows to the liver and gets converted to fat. One can of soda (~21g fructose) already blows past the lower threshold. Whole fruits with fiber sit comfortably under it for most types. Sugar remains bad, but the mechanism — and therefore the dose-dependent nuance — matters for practical choices.

Peter Attia

In a 400-episode retrospective, Attia walks through his biggest view changes. On keto: he spent 3 years in strict ketosis (avg morning BHB ~1.8–2 mmol) and found it beneficial — but has updated his belief about why it works. He no longer thinks ketosis works via insulin suppression; he now thinks it works the same way most restrictive diets work — by making it easier to control energy intake. He now considers hyperinsulinemia a passenger, not the driver, of metabolic dysfunction. On extended fasting: he did quarterly 7–10 day fasts plus monthly 3-day fasts for years on the theory that prolonged nutrient deprivation promotes enough autophagy to have real longevity benefits. He's since walked that back, citing both the cost (muscle loss, disruption) and the emerging evidence that the same autophagy signal can be achieved through exercise with far less downside. His overall posture has shifted from diet dogmatism toward protein sufficiency (≥1.4g/kg) as the non-negotiable, with the macro split mattering much less than once believed.

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz flags a recent major cardiology trial finding that lowering Lp(a) did not improve heart attack and stroke outcomes — undermining the simple "high Lp(a) = high risk" framing most doctors use. He argues what actually matters is the metabolic environment around Lp(a): research shows that in people with low waist-to-hip ratios, high Lp(a) is not significantly associated with increased cardiovascular risk. He also cites Linus Pauling's hypothesis that Lp(a) is an evolutionary stand-in for vitamin C, suggesting high-dose vitamin C may be cardioprotective specifically in people with high Lp(a).

🧬 Health & Science

The Ekberg and DeLauer items above cover the most substantive health science this cycle. A few additional specific claims worth noting:

Peter Attia on Extended Fasting — The Updated Position

Attia's reversal on prolonged fasting is worth sitting with: he did quarterly 7–10 day fasts for ~3 years, primarily to drive autophagy. His updated view is that (a) we don't have a unit of measure for autophagy in humans the way we do for ketosis, (b) the human evidence for autophagy benefits from extended fasting remains weak compared to preclinical models, and (c) the cost — particularly muscle protein loss — may not be worth it when exercise can produce meaningful autophagy signals with far less downside. He now treats protein sufficiency (≥1.4g/kg body weight) as the nutritional non-negotiable, with the macro split being largely flexible around that constraint. This is a meaningful update from someone who was a highly visible early advocate for extended fasting.

Peter Attia — Strong Convictions, Loosely Held (Episode 400)

Lp(a) Trial Result: Lowering the Biomarker Didn't Reduce Events

Nick Norwitz flags that a major recent cardiology trial found lowering Lp(a) levels did not improve cardiovascular outcomes (heart attacks/strokes). This is clinically significant because Lp(a) has been treated as a key modifiable risk factor, driving expensive interventions. Norwitz's interpretation: what matters is the surrounding metabolic context — specifically abdominal fat and waist-to-hip ratio — rather than the Lp(a) number in isolation. He cites research showing high Lp(a) in people with low waist-to-hip ratios carries no significant excess risk.

Stoic Premeditation of Anger

Donald Robertson writes on the Stoic practice of preemptively visualizing situations likely to trigger anger — essentially a cognitive rehearsal technique — as preventative therapy against the desire for revenge. The Stoics (particularly Seneca in De Ira) treated anger as the most destructive passion precisely because it masquerades as justice. Robertson frames this as a practical technique: running through anticipated provocations in the morning as a kind of emotional inoculation before you encounter them.
