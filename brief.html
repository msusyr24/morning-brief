Worth your time:

Thomas DeLauer

DeLauer's fasting video with Joel Greene is the most intellectually interesting health content this cycle. Greene's core argument: fasting is beneficial partly because it feeds Akkermansia muciniphila, a gut bacterium that strengthens the mucus lining — but on a low-fiber or carnivore diet with extended fasting, Akkermansia has no polyphenols or resistant starch to eat, so it defaults to consuming the gut lining itself. A 2026 Swedish study (n=4,200) found elevated Akkermansia levels combined with low-fiber diets correlated with higher type 2 diabetes risk — the opposite of what most fasting advocates would expect. Greene frames this with a "U-shaped curve" and "time inversion effect" — the idea that most biological interventions are good in the Goldilocks zone but degrade over extended use, a useful framework applicable beyond fasting. His prescription: if you're going to fast and eat low-fiber, you're removing the check on Akkermansia's appetite.

DeLauer's protein video is also worth a skim: the key mechanism is leucine as the actual trigger for mTOR/muscle protein synthesis, requiring 2–3g per meal (roughly 20–25g high-quality animal protein). It's a threshold, not a dose-response — falling just short means the switch doesn't flip regardless of total daily protein. A study in Journal of Physiology showed that 80g of whey spaced as 20g every 3 hours produced 30–50% more muscle than the same protein taken as 10g every 90 min or 40g every 6 hours. For people over 40, "anabolic resistance" raises the leucine threshold — the fix isn't more total protein but more leucine per meal, or brief resistance training before eating to resensitize the pathway.

Jared Alderman

Alderman makes a sharp conceptual point about passive lines: there's a psychological bias in poker where aggression feels like agency and checking feels like passivity, which causes players to bet small as "insurance" to try to end hands early — effectively paying a premium to avoid uncertainty rather than maximizing EV. His argument is that versus recreational players, the instinct to "establish control" by betting is often just fear of variance dressed up as competence. He also pushes back on overly simple opponent models — a player who "check-raises top pair every time" is playing aggressively but with no bluffs, and the correct response is categorically different from how you'd respond to a balanced aggressor. The diagnostic question: can you articulate the specific contours of your opponent's strategy, not just a one-word label?

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz covers a new Nature study on bowhead whale longevity. The paradox: bowhead whales have ~1,000x more cells than humans, live 200+ years, and yet their individual cells become cancerous after fewer mutations than human cells require — they should be swimming tumors. The resolution: whales have dramatically superior DNA repair machinery, centered on a protein called CIRBP (cold-inducible RNA binding protein). When researchers genetically engineered flies to produce whale CIRBP and then blasted them with X-ray radiation, some flies survived 100% longer than controls. Critically, humans already produce CIRBP — just at much lower levels. The authors state explicitly that contrary to assumptions, DNA repair can be improved in humans, and the whale provides proof of concept. The translational path (gene editing, peptide therapies to upregulate CIRBP) is real and closer than most realize, with CRISPR already being used clinically for sickle cell disease.

🧬 Health & Science

Fasting + Low-Fiber Diets: The Akkermansia Problem

Joel Greene's argument (via DeLauer's channel) is the most nuanced fasting critique in a while and grounded in a real mechanism. Akkermansia muciniphila normally splits its food source between dietary polyphenols/resistant starch and gut mucus; remove the dietary sources via carnivore + extended fasting and you've created a monoculture that eats your gut lining with no competitive check. A 2026 Swedish study (n=4,200) found elevated Akkermansia + low-fiber diet correlated with increased type 2 diabetes risk — the opposite of what the "Akkermansia is always good" narrative predicts. The practical implication: if you're doing extended fasting on a very low-fiber diet, you may be degrading the gut lining over time even while other metabolic markers look good. Greene's proposed solution mirrors what ancestral foraging would have provided: active movement during fasting periods and periodic consumption of polyphenol-rich or resistant-starch foods to give Akkermansia an alternative food source.

Leucine Threshold for Muscle Protein Synthesis: The Actual Mechanism

Thomas DeLauer synthesizes Don Layman's work cleanly: leucine specifically activates mTORC1 (the master switch for muscle building), and the threshold is approximately 2–3g per meal — a hard cutoff, not a dose-response curve. Below it, essentially no muscle protein synthesis is triggered regardless of other amino acids present. Whey protein has ~10–11g leucine per 100g protein vs. 8g for beef/chicken/eggs, which is why whey triggers the switch more efficiently per gram. The critical practical point: spreading the same 80g of protein as 20g every 3 hours produces 30–50% more muscle than grazing (10g/90 min) or infrequent large boluses (40g/6 hrs) — because you need to repeatedly hit the threshold and then let it reset. For people over 40, anabolic resistance raises the threshold, but brief resistance exercise before eating resensitizes the pathway, and adding leucine supplementation to lower-leucine meals can compensate for reduced muscle responsiveness.

Fructose Metabolism: The Liver-First Story Was Wrong

Sten Ekberg covers a 2018 discovery (confirmed in follow-up studies) that overturns the Lustig framework: fructose is processed first by the small intestine, not the liver. At low doses (~10–25g per sitting in humans, though exact thresholds are uncertain), ~90% is cleared by the gut and converted to glucose and lactate. The liver only handles overflow. This makes the harm dose-dependent and speed-dependent — liquid fructose on an empty stomach saturates intestinal capacity at the low end of that range (~10g, or half a can of soda), while fructose in whole fruit with fiber and slower absorption can clear more without burdening the liver. The practical takeaway: fructose is still harmful at typical consumption levels (a 20oz soda delivers 36g, far above the threshold), and the satiety-disrupting mechanisms (ghrelin suppression failure, brain reward center activation, decision-making impairment vs. glucose) remain fully intact regardless of first-pass location.

Bowhead Whale DNA Repair: A New Path to Cancer Prevention

A new Nature study (covered by Nick Norwitz) identifies CIRBP — cold-inducible RNA binding protein — as a key mechanism behind bowhead whales' cancer resistance despite having 1,000x more cells than humans and living 200+ years. The paradox: whale cells are individually more vulnerable to becoming cancerous (require fewer mutations) yet whales don't get cancer, because their superior DNA repair machinery catches errors before they accumulate. When flies were engineered to produce whale or even human CIRBP at higher levels and then irradiated, survival doubled. The authors directly challenge the assumption that DNA repair cannot be meaningfully improved in humans — we produce CIRBP already, just at lower levels. The thymus finding is a useful companion point: 2026 research shows adults with better-preserved thymic function have measurably lower cancer incidence, and thymus rejuvenation via growth hormone signaling pathways is an active research area.
