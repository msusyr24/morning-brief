GTO Wizard

Tom Boss 21 makes a compelling empirical case for late registration in MTTs that goes beyond the usual ICM theory. The core finding: using actual WSOP data, late registrants saw 10–15% ROI boosts — larger than most players assumed. He then controlled for skill using Triton data (2025–26, ~8,000 bullets), comparing each player's late entries against their own earlier bullets, and found that later entries still outperformed by 10–13%. Critically, ICM-predicted variance doesn't increase with late reg, meaning the risk-adjusted return genuinely improves — which inverts the conventional wisdom that you need a bigger bankroll to max late reg. He also isolates a specific failure mode: at bullet 5+, ROI collapses to roughly –40%, which he attributes to tilt rather than any structural disadvantage.

Jared Alderman

A concise framework for multiway spots: before looking at your hand, classify the board as one that punishes betting or punishes checking. On dry, high-card boards (e.g., A72) where your range is condensed and opponents are unlikely to lead enough, checking costs you less — so you can afford to be selective about what you bet. On connected, low boards (e.g., 876) where opponents are likely to under-lead their correct frequency, betting into unchecked aggression gets worse, meaning you need stronger incentives to bet and should be more precise about hand selection.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia: Multifactorial Trials — What They Can and Can't Prove

Attia's piece addresses a critical methodological issue in health research: multifactorial trials test a bundle of interventions simultaneously (e.g., diet + exercise + medication), which can show that the bundle works but can't isolate which component is driving the effect. This matters because it leads to widespread over-attribution — a trial showing "lifestyle intervention X" improved outcomes tells you almost nothing about which part of X to actually adopt. He argues this distinction is underappreciated both by researchers and the public consuming the results.

Peter Attia MD

Peter Attia Podcast: Hormones in Psychiatry — Specific Mechanisms

From the Drive episode transcript: SSRIs suppress dopamine and norepinephrine as a downstream effect of raising serotonin (via receptor upregulation/downregulation), which explains the "feeling better but blunted" phenomenon that makes long-term compliance difficult even in successful cases. The guest distinguishes "pure" SSRIs (escitalopram/Lexapro) from those with secondary pharmacology (sertraline/Zoloft also mildly blocks dopamine reuptake). Lexapro is more effective for anxiety disorders than commonly recognized, and was originally developed for dysthymia (chronic low-grade depression) rather than major depressive episodes — a distinction with real clinical implications.

Forget the Good Life, Try the Good Day

Massimo Pigliucci (Figs in Winter) on a Stoic reframe: instead of optimizing for "the good life" as some distant achievement, focus on the good day as the only unit of time you actually control. The argument draws on Seneca's observation that we lose the present by constantly projecting into the future — and that the discipline of well-executed days is both the means and the end.

The Stoic Taxonomy of Anger

Donald Robertson lays out how the Stoics (primarily Seneca in De Ira) classified anger — distinguishing between the initial involuntary passion (a proto-emotion or "propathe"), the judgment that assents to it, and the full-blown emotion that results. The practical payoff: the Stoics weren't asking you to suppress the initial flinch, only to not ratify it with a value judgment. This is more actionable than most modern anger management advice and maps surprisingly well onto CBT.
