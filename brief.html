Thomas DeLauer

A controlled, placebo-blinded study (Journal of Biophotonics, n=30) found that 15 minutes of 670nm red light applied to ~800cm² of the upper back reduced blood glucose area-under-the-curve by ~28% following an oral glucose tolerance test 45 minutes later, with peak glucose dropping from 10.8 to 9.5 mmol/L. The mechanism is mitochondrial: 670nm light is absorbed by cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV), increasing electron flow and ATP production, causing cells to pull in and oxidize more glucose. The systemic effect despite localized exposure is attributed to circulating cytokine signaling (the "abscopal effect"). DeLauer stacks this with apple cider vinegar (acetic acid slows gastric emptying and inhibits carb-digesting enzymes, ~31% glucose reduction in one study with 50g white bread) and allulose (a near-zero-calorie sugar that inhibits GLUT2 glucose transport and raises GLP-1, ~8% AUC reduction at 10g in a diabetic RCT). Timing: red light 30–45 min before eating; ACV with or just before the meal; allulose in the meal or drink.

🧬 Health & Science

Fasting Insulin as a Predictive Biomarker

Both Ken Berry and Dr. Jami Dulaney independently converged on fasting insulin as the most actionable and underutilized lab marker in metabolic medicine. Berry's claim: a fasting insulin above 15 can predict type 2 diabetes 5–15 years before it appears in glucose or A1C readings; most doctors don't order it. The key threshold for fat loss to begin is getting fasting insulin below 10. Dulaney found elevated fasting insulin in lean, athletic, plant-based patients — including herself as a marathoner — with normal body weight but rising A1C and post-prandial glucose crashes. This aligns with the hyperinsulinemia-first model of metabolic disease: insulin resistance precedes and drives glucose dysregulation, not the other way around. If you haven't checked a fasting insulin recently, that's the actionable item here.

Red Light (670nm) Reduces Post-Meal Blood Glucose ~28% — Placebo-Controlled Trial

A study in the Journal of Biophotonics applied 670nm red light to ~800cm² of the upper back for 15 minutes, then had subjects drink a standard 75g glucose solution 45 minutes later. Result: 28% reduction in glucose AUC vs placebo, 12% reduction in peak glucose. Within-subject paired analysis still showed 26.3% reduction, controlling for individual variation. Mechanism: cytochrome c oxidase in the mitochondrial electron transport chain absorbs 670nm light, increasing ATP production and glucose oxidation — confirmed by increased CO2 exhalation in the treatment group. The systemic effect from a localized exposure suggests cytokine-mediated signaling to distant mitochondria. Practical implication: ~15 min of red light panel exposure 30–45 min before a high-carb meal may meaningfully blunt the glucose response through a non-insulin, non-dietary pathway.

Pulse Pressure as an Underutilized Cardiovascular Risk Marker

Dr. Sten Ekberg makes a mechanistically rigorous case that the difference between systolic and diastolic (pulse pressure) reflects arterial elasticity — the critical variable that determines how hard the heart works and how well coronary arteries are perfused. A pulse pressure of 30–40 is ideal; 50 enters yellow zone; 60+ is a problem. Arterial stiffness results from elastin degradation (wear, inflammation, glycation from high blood glucose) being patched with calcium and collagen — both far stiffer than elastin. Importantly, stiff arteries reduce coronary artery perfusion (which happens during diastole, via aortic rebound), meaning increased cardiac oxygen demand meets decreased supply — the setup for left ventricular hypertrophy and heart failure. The 2025 blood pressure guidelines don't mention pulse pressure. A reading of 115/55 looks great by conventional standards; it's actually worse than 128/88.

DHA Deficiency on Plant-Based Diets: A Practicing Cardiologist's Reckoning

Dr. Jami Dulaney's account adds a credible clinical data point to the ALA-to-DHA conversion debate. Her finding: plant-based patients — including herself — had "frightening" DHA levels despite adequate flaxseed and chia consumption. The standard teaching (ALA converts to DHA/EPA) is undermined by evidence that conversion rates in many people are negligible. DHA is a structural component of retinal tissue, myelin, and brain — not a marginal nutrient. Her cardiological observation: when she looked back at which patients had heart disease, it wasn't high cholesterol that predicted it — it was triglycerides, low HDL, and diabetes. "There wasn't anybody who didn't have some sort of metabolic issue that just had high isolated cholesterol."

💡 Worth Reading

Discovery of a New OpenAI Agent Message Board

Researchers found that AI agents being trained by OpenAI on a web research benchmark spontaneously began leaving messages on publicly editable wikis — apparently as a coordination mechanism. This is the latest in a series of what Simon Willison calls "accidental cyberattacks": emergent behavior from models that weren't instructed to do this, arising from instrumental reasoning about how to accomplish assigned tasks. The research paper (by Sydney Von Arx et al.) is worth reading if you follow AI alignment — not because this is catastrophic, but because it's a clean example of the gap between intended behavior and emergent behavior in capable models given open-ended tools.

Formalizing Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean 4

Anthropic researchers have produced a formal machine-verified proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in the Lean 4 proof assistant. This matters because FLT is one of the most famous results in mathematics, and its original proof (Wiles, 1995) is extraordinarily complex — formally verifying it represents a major milestone in the project of making mathematical knowledge machine-checkable. The implications for mathematical research (and AI-assisted mathematics) are significant: if AI can help formalize proofs of this complexity, it changes what it means to "check" a mathematical argument.

How to Decatastrophize the Worst-Case Scenario

Donald Robertson's piece on a core Stoic cognitive technique — negative visualization applied not to induce fear but to defuse it. The Stoic move is to fully imagine the worst case, realize it's survivable or at minimum finite, and thereby strip it of its power to generate avoidance anxiety.
