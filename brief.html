Anthony Chaffee MD

Chaffee ranked carnivore foods by nutritional quality with some genuinely useful specifics. His top tier: wild-caught/hunted animals and grass-fed/finished ruminants — but only if you keep the fat. He cites a 2020 Journal of the American College of Cardiology meta-analysis (Astrup et al.) finding no increased cardiovascular risk from saturated fat, and actually protective effects against stroke. 🧬 Health & Science

Creatine's Methylation Cost — A Mechanism Worth Knowing

Thomas DeLauer covers underreported creatine biochemistry: your body's creatine synthesis consumes roughly 40% of all SAM-derived methyl groups — more than every other methyltransferase reaction in the body combined, per a study in the American Journal of Physiology. SAM is the primary methyl donor for DNA methylation, dopamine/serotonin synthesis, and liver detoxification. Supplementing creatine shuts down endogenous synthesis (confirmed in British Journal of Nutrition), freeing those methyl groups for other uses. A second cost: creatine synthesis burns glycine, and you're already running a ~5g/day glycine deficit (you need ~10g for collagen turnover; you make ~3g and get 2–5g from diet). Practical upshot: if you take collagen for joints/skin but not creatine, much of your glycine is getting diverted to creatine synthesis anyway. The homocysteine angle: creatine synthesis is responsible for a large share of homocysteine production via SAM → homocysteine byproduct; supplementing creatine measurably reduced GAA and lowered homocysteine in the AJP study. Dosing suggestion: 5g minimum, 10g if over 30 or dealing with connective tissue issues, split throughout the day.

Thomas DeLauer: Creatine Methylation Video

Fiber, IBD, and Carnivore — What the Data Actually Says

Nick Norwitz cites a 2012 study where constipation-IBS patients went from 4 bowel movements/day to 1/day (normalized) after eliminating dietary fiber — with less bloating and straining. More striking: he describes a published case series of 10 severe IBD patients (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's, some post-surgery, all failed conventional treatment) who responded "remarkably" to a zero-fiber carnivore diet. He also cites broader literature claiming fiber elimination causes remission in 60–85% of treatment-resistant IBD cases. He's careful to note this isn't a cure-all, but frames it as a legitimate clinical tool that deserves curiosity rather than dismissal — and notes "today's fringe is tomorrow's standard of care."

Nick Norwitz: When Fiber Harms the Gut

Lithium and Alzheimer's: Negative Trial, Open Questions

The LATTICE trial — a clinical trial of lithium for Alzheimer's disease — came back negative, but Peter Attia's analysis argues this doesn't close the book on low-dose lithium as a neuroprotective agent. The distinction matters: the trial may have tested the wrong dose, the wrong population, or intervened too late in disease progression. Low-dose lithium (drinking water trace amounts, not psychiatric doses) has epidemiological associations with lower dementia rates that remain unexplained. Worth tracking if you're following Alzheimer's prevention research.

Peter Attia: Lithium and Alzheimer's Disease

Hypertrophy and Strength: More Correlated in Beginners Than We Thought, But Still Not Predictive

Stronger by Science's new analysis finds prior studies underestimated how much muscle growth contributes to strength gains in newer lifters — but the relationship is still far from deterministic. Translation: hypertrophy training and strength training overlap more than some "specificity" arguments suggest, especially early in training. For advanced lifters, the relationship weakens considerably, and neural adaptations dominate. Practical: for beginners, chasing hypertrophy and strength simultaneously is less of a compromise than previously thought.

The Decline in the Transmission of Scientific Ideas

A new paper documents that the diffusion of scientific ideas across fields has declined substantially over the past 40 years, and links the cause to increasing specialization in scientific language — more technical terminology means fewer researchers in adjacent fields can absorb and build on the work. This is a real cost of hyperspecialization: science may be producing more knowledge while simultaneously transmitting less of it. The finding has implications for how you read research: the most cross-domain-useful science is probably in the most accessible papers, not the most technical ones.

Marginal Revolution

Design is compromise

A short, dense essay arguing that every design decision is a tradeoff — optimizing for one property necessarily weakens another — and that recognizing this explicitly makes you a better decision-maker in any domain. The framing applies well beyond product design: to training programming, to portfolio construction, to communication. The essay pushes back on the idea that there are "right answers" in design, replacing it with "best available tradeoffs given your constraints." Worth sitting with as a mental model.

Argues that as AI handles more execution, the scarce human skills become sustained attention and the ability to see a complex project through to completion — not raw creativity or domain knowledge. The piece reframes what it means to be "good at" working with AI: not prompting skill, but the cognitive discipline to stay oriented on a goal across many iterations and not get distracted by the AI's tendency to produce plausible-looking intermediate outputs. Rick Manelius

The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge (1939)

Abraham Flexner's 1939 essay arguing that the most practically valuable scientific breakthroughs have consistently come from researchers pursuing curiosity-driven "useless" knowledge with no application in mind — Maxwell's equations, Faraday's electromagnetic work, Pasteur's germ theory. The essay is a direct counterargument to purely applied research agendas and a defense of intellectual freedom. Short, elegant, and holds up completely. Worth an hour of your time if you haven't read it.
