Jared Alderman (feat. Uri Peleg)

Uri Peleg makes a sharp point that gets missed in most GTO discourse: it only matters if you're bluffing too much or too little if your opponent is actually adjusting to it. His implication is that you can over-bluff some boards, under-bluff others, and globally be fine — because there's no practical way for opponents to detect those imbalances unless you tell them. He also raises the underappreciated idea that your opponent doesn't know your game tree, so what "check" means, or what sizing you'll use, is hidden information you can exploit asymmetrically. The broader frame: GTO is built on the premise that both players know each other's strategy, but in practice that's never true — and that gap is where real edges live. The chess "superposition of chessboards" analogy for thinking about range construction across streets is genuinely useful.

Nick Norwitz

Solid walkthrough of nitric oxide's cardiovascular mechanisms — vasodilation via shear stress activating eNOS, anti-clotting via platelet inhibition, and anti-inflammatory effects on atherosclerotic plaque formation. Most actionable findings: regular mouthwash use kills oral bacteria that convert dietary nitrate to nitrite, raising blood pressure by ~2–3.5 mmHg (a small but cumulative effect he argues isn't worth it); nasal breathing vs. mouth breathing lowers blood pressure by ~4 mmHg; and humming can increase nasal nitric oxide production 15-fold over quiet exhalation by creating oscillating airflow through the paranasal sinuses. On the dietary side, ~500mg dietary nitrate (roughly one cup of beetroot) produced 12–14% improvements in endurance exercise tasks in a controlled trial. He also used AI to help design a low-carb nitric oxide-boosting brownie recipe, which is a fun demonstration of AI as a practical nutrition tool.

Thomas DeLauer

Makes a distinction that's genuinely underappreciated: fiber feeds gut bacteria, but it doesn't directly rebuild the intestinal lining — and if that lining is already compromised, fiber may actually irritate it further. The gut lining (a single cell thick, turning over every 3–5 days) requires glutamine as its primary fuel, glycine for collagen synthesis supporting the connective tissue framework, and phosphatidylcholine for cell membrane rebuilding. He argues most gut protocols address the microbiome side while ignoring the structural repair side, and the best food lineup for the latter is bone broth (glycine, collagen peptides), egg yolks (phosphatidylcholine), quality animal protein (glutamine precursors), fermented dairy, and omega-3s — not fiber-first approaches. The brain fog connection: a damaged gut lining allows LPS (bacterial fragments) into circulation, triggering cytokines that cross the blood-brain barrier and manifest as cognitive slowness and emotional flatness before digestive symptoms even appear.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia on Emotion Neuroscience (Lisa Feldman Barrett, Ph.D.)

Barrett's core thesis (from the RSS feed item, episode #413): emotions are not hardwired reactions triggered by events — they are predictions your brain constructs based on past experience and bodily signals, then uses to make sense of incoming sensory data. The practical implication is significant: if emotions are constructed predictions rather than automatic responses, you have more leverage over them than the "emotions just happen to me" model suggests. This maps well onto Stoic ideas about the gap between stimulus and response, but with a neuroscientific mechanism underneath. The specific claim worth sitting with: "What is an emotion? It's a set of predictions that tells a story about what a set of sensory signals mean to you in that situation."

The Drive #413 — Lisa Feldman Barrett

Ken Berry: A1C Targets and Low-Carb vs. Pharmaceutical Management

Berry's pointed critique of mainstream diabetes guidance: associations like ADA take money from pharma and food manufacturers, leading to advice (eat whole grains, maintain A1C ~7.5) that is internally contradictory — you cannot eat a high-carb diet AND maintain a normal A1C as a diabetic. Pharmaceutical attempts to hit lower A1C targets while eating carbs create hypoglycemia risk, which in elderly patients causes falls, broken hips, and subdural hematomas. His evidence-based alternative: very low-carb or carnivore diets allow Type 2 diabetics to normalize A1C with zero hypo risk and, in his clinical experience, achieve full remission. Type 1 diabetics on very low-carb diets in studies inject 50–70% less insulin and achieve more stable glucose.

Epictetus Argues Against the Death Penalty

Massimo Pigliucci's close reading of Discourses 1.18 — Epictetus's argument that wrongdoing stems from ignorance (people do wrong because they falsely believe it will benefit them), and therefore punishment should aim at correction, not retribution. The Stoic position is that no one chooses evil knowingly — they choose what they believe to be good and are simply mistaken. This has real implications for how you think about anger toward people who wrong you: they are, in a Stoic frame, confused rather than malicious.

Stoic Premeditation of Anger

Donald Robertson on the Stoic practice of mentally rehearsing likely provocations before they happen — essentially preventative cognitive therapy for anger. The key Stoic insight here: anger is most dangerous in its first moments, before reason can engage, and premeditating on likely frustrations weakens their initial grip. Pairs naturally with Robertson's piece on Rational Emotive Imagery (also out this week), which is a structured visualization practice for updating emotional responses to specific beliefs.
