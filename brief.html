GTO Wizard (Andrew Brokos)

Andrew Brokos delivers a genuinely rigorous framework for understanding when and why to bet. The core insight: betting accomplishes two things — growing the pot and denying equity/EV — and every hand sits on a spectrum between deriving value primarily from one or the other. The counterintuitive takeaway is that Ace-Four of hearts (top pair + nut flush draw) bets less frequently than it seems it should, because it doesn't benefit much from folds (already ahead) and doesn't barrel enough turns to justify growing the pot on the flop. Queen-Nine of hearts, by contrast, bets more because it has many profitable turn barrels (flush draw pickups, backdoor outs), making pot-growth valuable even without showdown equity. Medium-strength hands like pocket queens are the least frequent betters — they gain little from folds and don't want a large pot either. GTO Lab's short reinforces this by noting the importance of reading equity distribution tiers before studying solver actions.

Dr. Sten Ekberg

Ekberg makes the case — with actual metabolic mechanisms — for why walking often outperforms running for fat loss specifically (not calorie burn generally). At low intensity (~56% max heart rate), the body burns roughly 80% fat and 20% carbs; at 80% max heart rate, that flips to ~25% fat and 75% carbs. More importantly, running triggers sharp cortisol spikes (which increase cravings and suppress fat oxidation), a large glucagon surge that depletes glycogen and signals the body to refuel aggressively, and a hard ghrelin rebound post-workout. Walking produces minimal versions of all three responses, meaning fewer post-exercise cravings and better dietary adherence. Critically, people on low-carb/high-fat diets can shift their fat-max zone dramatically upward — potentially maintaining fat dominance even at 80–90% max intensity — so the optimal intensity is highly individual and best calibrated by monitoring hunger and cravings response.

Thomas DeLauer

A UC Davis study tested 54 avocado oil products (mayo, chips, dressings) using gas chromatography and plant sterol fingerprinting — two independent tests. 48 of 54 showed adulteration with seed oils, and the researchers deliberately required >10% variance before flagging a product. One product was 100% soybean oil. The "database is outdated" defense doesn't hold: avocados have a stable fatty acid and phytosterol profile regardless of cultivar or year, and the researchers confirmed the methodology by heating confirmed avocado oil and showing its profile didn't change. Unlike olive oil (which is regulated), avocado oil has almost no regulatory oversight. The guest scientist notes this is primarily an ethics/cheating problem enabled by a few upstream suppliers, analogous to the chocolate/cadmium contamination pattern.
