Peter Attia

In his AMA reflecting on 8 years of the podcast, Peter walks through his biggest belief reversals. Nick Norwitz

Norwitz walks through a large fMRI study (~12,000 children) showing that stimulant medications (Adderall, Ritalin) don't act on attention networks at all — they act on sensory/motor arousal systems and reward circuits, leaving attention circuitry untouched. This resolves two paradoxes: (1) why stimulants help underperformers but not high-performers (Yerkes-Dodson curve — you push under-aroused people toward the peak, but push already-optimal people into jittery over-arousal); (2) why stimulants calm hyperactive kids (they're increasing arousal that mimics what good sleep provides, not suppressing activity). The practical implication: in children with delayed circadian phase (melatonin comes online late, want to sleep at 2am but forced awake at 7am), stimulants may be functioning as a sleep-deprivation patch rather than treating a true attention disorder. Only 48% of children in the study got recommended sleep. His actionable takeaway: fix the sleep clock first — light exposure timing, sleep hygiene — before assuming ADHD diagnosis is primary.

Robert Greene (Modern Wisdom)

Greene's new book on the Sublime, seven years in the making, argues that adults progressively lose the child's unfiltered sensory openness — the infant brain has no inhibitory filtering, everything is novel; adult brains suppress ~90% of sensory input to enable planning and survival. The problem: modern life (social media algorithms, constant stimulation) accelerates this flattening while removing the transcendent experiences that historically gave life depth. Greene's claim is that people seeking the Sublime through porn, drugs, Marvel movies, or political outrage are pursuing false versions of what the Sublime actually delivers — genuine encounter with something larger than the self. His prescription isn't to abandon function but to create deliberate windows: unplugged time in nature, genuine curiosity about other people's inner lives, sitting with mortality rather than avoiding it.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia: Keto Works, But Not For The Reason We Thought

Attia's core reversal (from his AMA): he no longer believes ketogenic diets work through insulin suppression. His updated model is that hyperinsulinemia is a downstream consequence of energy imbalance, not a cause of fat accumulation — and that keto works because it improves satiety and makes caloric restriction easier, not because of any unique metabolic magic. The clinical implication: you can gain weight on keto if you're eating too much, and you can lose weight on almost any diet if you control energy intake and hit protein targets (≥1.4g/kg). This makes him much less prescriptive about dietary strategy and much more focused on protein adequacy and long-term adherence.

Peter Attia AMA #90 · YouTube

Fructose Mechanism Corrected: The Liver Is the Backup, Not the First Stop

A 2018 mouse study (confirmed in follow-up human-consistent research) showed the small intestine processes fructose first, handling ~90% at low doses before passing overflow to the liver. The critical threshold appears to be roughly 10-25g fructose per sitting in humans, with liquid sugar (soda, juice) hitting the lower bound due to fast absorption and whole fruit with fiber hitting the upper. Below the threshold, the liver is largely spared. Above it — one can of Coke (21g fructose), one 20oz bottle (36g) — the liver takes the full overflow load. The satiety problem remains independent of this mechanism: fructose suppresses ghrelin less than glucose, triggers almost no insulin response, and lights up brain reward centers more intensely, driving continued eating.

Dr. Sten Ekberg

ADHD Stimulants May Be Patching Sleep Deprivation, Not Fixing Attention

fMRI data from ~12,000 children showed stimulant medications activate sensory/motor arousal and reward networks — not attention circuits, which were left unchanged. The practical implication from the data: stimulants only improved grades in sleep-deprived children; well-rested children showed no benefit. The hypothesis is that a meaningful fraction of children prescribed stimulants have delayed circadian phase (want to sleep at 2am, forced awake at 7am), creating chronic sleep debt that presents identically to ADHD symptoms. Stimulants then function as a sleep-debt compensator rather than an attention enhancer. Only 48% of children in the study met recommended sleep guidelines.

Nick Norwitz

Cardio Minimum: 3 × 20 Minutes Hard Per Week; Lifting Alone Insufficient for Long-Term Heart Health

RP's analysis: heavy lifting (5-15 rep range, long rests) does not meaningfully replace cardio for cardiovascular health — the cardiorespiratory demand is too low. However, 15-30 rep sets with minimal rest on large compound movements does approximate cardio and is the most time-efficient hybrid. Without dedicated cardio, blood pressure and glucose control drift negatively over years and decades, not weeks — so you won't feel it immediately but it accumulates. The minimum effective dose to "green check box" cardio is 3 weekly sessions of 20 minutes at high effort (can't talk intensity). 8-12K daily steps handles insulin sensitivity and most of cardio's acute benefits; dedicated cardio adds the long-term cardiovascular-specific benefit on top.

💡 Worth Reading

The Baffling Intelligence of a Single Cell (2024)

An essay on E. coli chemotaxis — how a bacterium with no brain navigates chemical gradients by essentially doing temporal averaging: comparing current receptor stimulation to recent memory of stimulation to decide whether things are getting better or worse, then adjusting its tumbling frequency accordingly. The piece is a meditation on how extraordinarily sophisticated "dumb" biological systems are when you actually look at the mechanism, and by extension, how much intelligence we may be misattributing to centralized nervous systems. High-scoring on HN (296 pts) and worth 20 minutes.

Stoicism and Wealth

Pigliucci examines the apparent tension between Stoic philosophy and external goods like wealth — the Stoic position is that wealth is a "preferred indifferent" (prokegmenon): it's not bad to have, not required for eudaimonia, but also not something to organize your life around. The practical upshot is a framework for holding financial success without being held by it — which is harder than it sounds and worth sitting with if you're in a phase of accumulating.
