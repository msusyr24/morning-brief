🎥 From People I Follow

Worth your time:

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz makes a compelling, mechanism-grounded case that the vagus nerve acts as a literal anti-inflammatory circuit connecting brain to gut — and that you can consciously modulate it. The anchor study: stressed mice with severe IBD died within days, while vagus nerve electrical stimulation rescued them and dropped inflammatory markers (IL-6, TNF-alpha) by ~70% via a molecular process called SUMOylation. In humans, a 242-patient randomized double-blind trial (RESET-R) found that an implanted vagus nerve stimulator significantly improved rheumatoid arthritis — a condition that had already failed multiple drugs — and slowed joint erosion on MRI, earning FDA approval in 2025. The practical free tool he emphasizes: cyclic sighing (double inhale through nose, long slow exhale through mouth), which a Stanford RCT found outperformed mindfulness meditation for lowering physiologic arousal.

Video: How to Use Your Vagus Nerve to Improve Gut Health

Peter Attia

Attia's AMA on thyroid is a rare clinical-nuance piece worth absorbing. His core framework: thyroid dysfunction follows a Goldilocks principle (too much and too little are both serious problems), and mismanagement runs in both directions — dismissing real hypothyroidism because TSH falls in the "normal" range (e.g., 4.2 while the patient has a patchy gland and positive antibodies), and overtreating subclinical findings in functional medicine settings where borderline free T3 or elevated reverse T3 becomes "irrefutable proof" regardless of TSH. Key mechanistic point: T4 (the inactive prohormone secreted by the thyroid) must convert to active T3 locally in each tissue via different enzymes — TSH tells you what the gland is doing, not what's happening in the liver, brain, or heart. He flags that overt thyroid hormone treatment is not benign: it can cause cognitive symptoms, atrial fibrillation, and bone loss.

Video: Thyroid health: interpreting symptoms, diagnosing & treating dysfunction · Podcast: AMA #89

🧬 Health & Science

Vagus Nerve Stimulation as Anti-Inflammatory Intervention (Nick Norwitz)

The mechanistic picture Norwitz lays out is worth sitting with: vagus nerve tone is the brain's inflammation thermostat. When it's suppressed by stress, pro-inflammatory signals can more than triple and anti-inflammatory brakes fail — in animals, this turned manageable IBD lethal. Vagal stimulation reversed this by suppressing a molecular tagging process (SUMOylation) by ~70% in gut tissue. The human translation: an FDA-approved (2025) implanted vagus nerve stimulator improved rheumatoid arthritis in a 242-patient RCT even in patients who had failed multiple drugs, and slowed joint erosion on MRI. Free protocol: cyclic sighing (double nasal inhale, long oral exhale) proven in a Stanford RCT to outperform meditation for reducing physiologic arousal — Norwitz uses it specifically in airports and on planes where he's most stress-prone.

Thyroid: Why the Gray Zone Is So Commonly Mismanaged (Peter Attia)

Attia's specific clinical framework: TSH measures whether the pituitary is telling the gland to work harder, but it's one step removed from what's actually happening in target tissues. Symptoms alone cannot distinguish hypothyroid from euthyroid in blinded studies — the same fatigue, weight gain, and cognitive fog show up with iron deficiency, sleep deprivation, and perimenopause. His pragmatic criteria for treating: don't dismiss someone with a patchy gland, positive antibodies, family history, and a TSH of 4.2 just because it's "normal" — but also don't treat borderline free T3 or elevated reverse T3 as definitive without full clinical context, because unnecessary thyroid hormone causes a-fib, bone loss, and cognitive symptoms. Overt treatment has real downside risk.

Energy Flux vs. Caloric Restriction for Fat Loss (Thomas DeLauer)

An AJCN study tracking 200+ people over 2-3 years found the best predictor of fat gain wasn't high calorie intake — it was low energy flux (eating little AND moving little). Separate metabolic chamber studies in the British Journal of Nutrition found higher energy turnover produces better fat oxidation even at the same net caloric deficit. The mechanism: caloric restriction triggers metabolic downregulation (lower RMR, reduced collagen synthesis, impaired mitochondrial function, depleted antioxidant systems) while increased movement with adequate food keeps these systems active. Protein leverage hypothesis (University of Sydney): the body defends a protein intake target more tightly than fat or carb intake, so eating low-protein processed food drives overeating as the body hunts for that protein target — front-loading 30-50g protein at the first meal blunts this.
