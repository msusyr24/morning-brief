Worth your time:

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz covers a genuinely striking Nature study on bowhead whales — the second-largest animal on Earth, living 200+ years with ~1,000x more cells than humans, yet essentially cancer-free. The paradox: whale cells actually become cancerous after fewer mutations than human cells, not more. The answer lies in superior DNA repair, driven by a protein called CIRBP (cold-inducible RNA binding protein). Researchers genetically engineered flies to produce the whale version of this protein; those flies lived up to 100% longer and survived radiation-induced DNA damage dramatically better. Crucially, humans already produce CIRBP — just far less of it. He also covers emerging thymus-rejuvenation research: adults with better-preserved thymic function develop cancer at lower rates, and peptides like TB-500/CJC may support this, though causality isn't proven. The takeaway: cancer prevention via DNA repair enhancement is plausible and no longer theoretical — CRISPR has already cured sickle cell disease using similar logic.

Peter Attia

Attia interviews oncologist Siddhartha Mukherjee (author of The Emperor of All Maladies) for a sweeping update on cancer science. Key claims: (1) Inflammatory states are now understood as pro-carcinogenic, but we still lack a reliable biomarker — "there is no high cholesterol for cancer yet." (2) Hereditary risk is more complex than BRCA1/2; Mukherjee distinguishes "shove mutations" (BRCA1 = 9-10x elevated risk) from "nudge mutations" (hundreds of small-effect genes that compound). (3) Colorectal cancer rising in young people remains a genuine mystery. (4) CAR-T cell therapy is producing 15-20 year survivors in previously untreatable leukemias and lymphomas. (5) Metabolic therapies — exploiting the fact that cancer cells metabolize differently from normal cells — are an active and promising treatment frontier. (6) New drugs like Enhertu (breast cancer) and a Ras-targeting agent (pancreatic cancer) are extending survival in cancers that were essentially death sentences 10-15 years ago.

Jared Alderman

A conceptually sharp video on when passive lines (checking instead of betting) are correct and actively profitable — not just "playing small ball." Alderman's core argument: aggression is psychologically felt as agency and control, but in poker checking is structurally identical to betting as a decision; the real skill is recognizing when passive lines exploit opponents better than aggressive ones. He flags a common leak: betting small as "insurance" to take down a pot now is actually paying a premium to avoid a better EV situation — the equivalent of buying insurance on a +EV spot. He also pushes back on two-dimensional player reads ("aggro," "station"), arguing that even weak players have internal frameworks and micro-tendencies that reward careful mapping. The meta-point: respecting your opponent's actual game — whatever that game is — is a prerequisite for exploiting it.

🧬 Health & Science

Fructose Metabolism: The Lustig Mechanism Was Wrong

Dr. Sten Ekberg covers a 2018 finding (confirmed in follow-up research) that overturned the prevailing model of fructose metabolism. The old claim — still being taught at CME conferences — was that fructose can only be processed by the liver, overwhelming it and causing fatty liver and insulin resistance. The actual finding: at low doses, the small intestine clears ~90% of fructose first, converting it to glucose and lactate. The liver only handles overflow. The threshold appears to be roughly 10-25g of fructose per sitting (context-dependent: liquid sugar, empty stomach, and fast consumption lower the threshold toward 10g; whole food with fiber raises it toward 25g). A can of Coke delivers ~21g; apple juice ~14g; a large bottled soda ~36g. Practical implication: the mechanism is highly dose- and speed-dependent, which means fruit consumed slowly with fiber behaves very differently from liquid fructose on an empty stomach — even at the same gram count. Fructose's harm via hunger signaling (it barely suppresses ghrelin, doesn't trigger insulin satiety signals, and lights up brain reward centers more than glucose) remains valid regardless of the mechanism correction.

Gray-Market Peptides and Big Pharma's Hidden Fingerprints

Peter Attia's team traced the patent and development histories of 25 popular gray-market peptides (BPC-157, TB-500, etc.) and found that the vast majority have documented ties to pharmaceutical companies — they were developed, patented, or studied by pharma before entering the gray market, often because the compounds couldn't be profitably patented once they became widely known. This reframes the "pharma vs. peptides" framing entirely: these aren't underground discoveries; many are pharma orphans. Relevant if you're thinking about which peptides have actual mechanistic backing vs. which are pure speculation.

The Fire Retardant in the Seafood Chain

An Inside Climate News investigation into ethoxyquin — a chemical originally developed as a rubber stabilizer and fire retardant, now widely used as a preservative in fishmeal (which feeds farmed fish). It's banned for direct use in human food in the EU but enters the food chain through aquaculture feed. This is the kind of indirect contamination pathway that doesn't show up in standard food safety testing because it's several steps upstream.

Stoic Premeditation of Anger — Donald Robertson

Robertson covers the Stoic technique of praemeditatio applied specifically to anger — the practice of mentally rehearsing provocations in advance so that the emotional response is weakened when the real event occurs. The Stoics (especially Seneca) viewed anger as a three-stage process: the initial impression, the judgment that harm was done, and the impulse to retaliate. Only the second stage — the judgment — is under your control, and premeditation gives you a window to intervene there. Robertson also covers the Stoic view on revenge: seeking it is irrational because it doesn't undo harm and produces more. Practically useful for decision-making under provocation.
