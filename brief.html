Jared Alderman

Alderman makes a conceptual case for passive lines that's worth sitting with: the bias toward betting as "taking control" is a psychological artifact, not a strategic one. Betting small doesn't guarantee taking down the pot — your opponent defends a wide range — and it costs you real equity in spots where a check-call line extracts more. More useful framing: even weak opponents play a structured game with internal rules (e.g., always check-raise top pair, never bluff certain boards), and labeling them one-dimensionally ("station," "aggro") blinds you to exploitable patterns. The smarter question is: what does this specific player never do, and what do they always do, and how does that reshape my line?

Dr. Sten Ekberg

Ekberg covers a significant 2018 update to fructose metabolism that overturns the widely-cited Lustig "fructose only hits the liver" model. New finding: the small intestine processes fructose first, clearing ~90% of low-dose fructose into glucose and lactate before it ever reaches the liver — the liver is the backup, not the primary processor. The critical variable is dose and speed: the gut saturates quickly, estimated at roughly 10–25g of fructose per sitting in humans, with the lower end applying to liquid sugar consumed fast on an empty stomach. A single can of soda (~21g fructose) or a 20oz bottle (~36g) exceeds that threshold entirely. Below the threshold, the liver is largely spared; above it, the overflow gets converted to fat. The practical implication: whole fruit with fiber (blackberries at 2.4g/100g fructose, strawberries 2.7g) stays safely under the gut's capacity, but even OJ (~10g/8oz) hits the cusp. Medjool dates are the outlier at 32g fructose per 100g — a single serving overwhelms the system regardless of context. Fructose also fails to suppress ghrelin meaningfully, doesn't produce an insulin spike, and lights up brain reward circuits more than glucose — the hunger-suppression pathway simply doesn't fire.

Peter Attia

In this 400-episode retrospective, Attia explicitly walks back his prior dogmatism on keto and extended fasting. On keto: he no longer believes the mechanism is insulin suppression — he now thinks it works for the same reason most restrictive diets work, which is that it reduces energy intake, often without deliberate calorie counting. Hyperinsulinemia travels with obesity but isn't the root cause; fixing insulin without fixing energy balance doesn't fix the problem, and you can gain weight on keto if you're in a caloric surplus. On extended fasting (he ran quarterly 7–10 day fasts for 3 years): the cost-benefit calculus shifted. The autophagy case relied on ~3 days being the threshold for meaningfully elevated autophagy beyond what exercise produces, but the evidence for clinical benefit in healthy humans never materialized to match the cost. His current framework: protein minimum (1.4g/kg bodyweight) is non-negotiable; dietary strategy beyond that is largely interchangeable as long as the protein target is met and energy balance is managed.

Fructose Metabolism Update: The Liver Is the Backup, Not the Primary Processor

The 2018 finding (confirmed in follow-up human research) that the small intestine clears ~90% of low-dose fructose before it reaches the liver upends 17 years of conventional framing. The dose-dependence is the actionable insight: at roughly 10–25g fructose per sitting (lower end for liquid sugar on an empty stomach, higher end for whole fruit eaten slowly with fiber), the gut capacity saturates and overflow hits the liver for fat conversion. A regular can of soda at ~21g and a 20oz bottle at ~36g both exceed the threshold substantially. Whole fruit with fiber — especially berries (2.4–2.7g fructose/100g) — stays under it. Medjool dates (32g/100g) are uniquely problematic. The satiety mechanism compounds this: fructose doesn't suppress ghrelin, doesn't trigger insulin, and activates brain reward centers more intensely than glucose, driving cravings and impaired impulse control around food.

Sixteen AI agents were each given 1,000 satoshis of real Bitcoin and told nothing about how to use it. Marginal Revolution

Stoic Premeditation of Anger

Donald Robertson covers the Stoic technique of premeditation applied specifically to anger — anticipating the situations and provocations that trigger it before they arise, so the emotional response is less automatic. The frame is preventative therapy rather than reactive recovery: Seneca's argument that by the time anger arrives, reason has already lost the battle, so the only reliable intervention is upstream, before the trigger. Practically, this connects to the Stoic principle that we suffer more in imagination than in reality — but anger inverts this, hitting hardest in the moment.

Conceptual Stretching: Why Everyone You Dislike Is a Fascist, or a Communist, or Both

Massimo Pigliucci (Figs in Winter) applies the political science concept of "conceptual stretching" — the habit of expanding the definition of a term until it loses analytical usefulness — to how people misuse "fascist" and "communist" as all-purpose insults. This is a mental models piece as much as a political one: when a concept's extension grows too large, it loses its intension, and you can no longer think clearly with it. The practical implication is about epistemic hygiene — sloppy political language produces sloppy political thinking.
