Reciprocity Tendency

When someone does you a small favor, you feel pressure to return it — often with interest. Hare Krishnas used this by giving flowers at airports before asking for donations. The cost of a flower vs. the guilt of not reciprocating was wildly asymmetric. Aware of this tendency, you can watch for it in negotiations, sales pitches, and favors-exchanged-for-favors politics.

Peter Attia (The Drive Podcast)

Attia sits down with cardiologist and lipidologist Michael Davidson, who argues that the window to prevent heart disease is the 20s and 30s — not after a cardiac event. Davidson uses the "8-gram rule": 200 mg/dL LDL × 40 years = 8 grams of cumulative cholesterol exposure, the threshold associated with heart disease. Keep LDL below 80 mg/dL for a lifetime and you largely don't get ASCVD. Davidson shares a compelling personal n=1: he started statins in medical school after his father died at 47; his brother, equally high-risk, chose strict vegetarianism and no statins until his mid-40s, and had bypass surgery at 44. Both Attia and Davidson are frustrated that LDL is treated differently than blood pressure or blood sugar — no one waits for a stroke to treat hypertension, yet physicians routinely delay lipid intervention. New pharmaceutical angles (including CETPi obicetrapib and emerging mechanisms) are discussed as reasons to be optimistic about reaching very low LDL without statins alone.

🧬 Health & Science

Early LDL Intervention: The 8-Gram Rule (Peter Attia / Michael Davidson)

Davidson's "8-gram lifetime LDL exposure" framework is the most actionable reframe from the Attia podcast: total cardiovascular risk is better modeled as cumulative LDL-years than 10-year risk. At 80 mg/dL LDL sustained across a lifetime, the data suggest near-zero ASCVD. This reframes the question from "should a 35-year-old take a statin?" to "why would you accumulate unnecessary LDL-years in your 30s when plaque formation is actively occurring?" The Davidson n=1 (two brothers, same high-risk genetics, one treated early, one later — early-treated avoided bypass at 44) illustrates the compounding effect of early intervention in a way that probabilistic risk scores don't convey. He also notes that genetics explain most elevated LDL cases seen in his lipid clinic, making lifestyle-only approaches insufficient for many patients.

Lemon Juice & Glucose Blunting: Salivary Amylase Mechanism (Thomas DeLauer)

A 2014 randomized crossover study (European Journal of Clinical Nutrition, n=17) found lemon juice alongside 100g bread reduced peak blood glucose by 30% and shifted the spike from 41 to 78 minutes. The mechanism: lemon juice's acidity prematurely inhibits salivary amylase, reducing starch breakdown from ~50% to a lower fraction before the small intestine — meaning larger, unabsorbable starch chains pass through rather than entering circulation rapidly. This was confirmed by showing pH-neutralized lemon juice cut the effect in half, isolating acidity (not polyphenols) as the primary driver. A separate rodent study showed lemon polyphenols upregulated PPARα, the nuclear receptor that drives fat adaptation during fasting. DeLauer's practical protocol: lemon juice at last meal of the day to smooth glucose/insulin response entering the overnight fast; or first thing in the morning in a fasted state to augment fat oxidation.

Fructose's Missing Off-Switch (Dr. Sten Ekberg)

Ekberg gives a mechanistically clean explanation of why fructose is metabolically worse than glucose beyond just calorie content: glucose metabolism via hexokinase produces a feedback signal that slows processing (negative feedback loop), while fructose metabolism via fructokinase has no such feedback and no ghrelin suppression — meaning hunger is unaffected regardless of how much fructose is consumed. The practical implication is that fruit juice (apple juice: ~110g sugar/L, ~60g fructose) is metabolically similar to Mountain Dew (~70g fructose/L) and should not be treated as health food. He also estimates 88–93% of the population has some degree of fatty liver infiltration due to the dose-dependent relationship between insulin resistance and hepatic fat accumulation.
