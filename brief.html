₿ Bitcoin & Macro

Bitcoin Blasts Past $68,000 After US Treasury Doubles Debt Buybacks

Bitcoin jumped ~3% to above $68,000 after the U.S. Treasury announced it would more than double the size of its government debt repurchase program. The macro read here is straightforward: active debt monetization signals are being interpreted by the market as dollar-dilutive, and Bitcoin is responding as a monetary hedge — consistent with the Saylor thesis that sovereign debt management is structurally bullish for hard assets. Separately, $1.7B in short positions were liquidated in the same move, suggesting significant leveraged bearish positioning got caught.

Bitcoin's Volatility May Be Down But Expect a 'Meaningful Move' Soon, Says Fidelity

Fidelity and VanEck research both flagged that Bitcoin's realized volatility has compressed significantly — historically a coiling pattern that precedes large directional moves. This is a data point worth tracking: low-volatility consolidation phases in prior cycles have resolved both up and down, but the macro backdrop (Treasury buybacks, potential CLARITY Act legislation) skews the asymmetry.

🧬 Health & Science

Mental Health Beyond Neurotransmitters: Hormones, Symptom Reduction, and Psychedelics

Peter Attia's guest Dr. Linus Abrams argues that psychiatry's dominant framework — targeting neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine) — is too narrow because it optimizes for symptom reduction rather than restoration of full human experience. His organizing claim: hormonal systems (thyroid, sex hormones, cortisol axis) are deeply underappreciated drivers of psychiatric states, meaning a patient who is "not depressed" on an SSRI may still be far from thriving if underlying hormonal dysregulation is untreated. The psychedelics discussion focuses on their potential to create plasticity windows, not just mood shifts, which is a distinct mechanistic argument from the serotonin theory.

Peter Attia MD

Ben Felix on Health Compounding: VO2 Max, Muscle Mass, and Longevity Data

From the Ben Felix video flagged above: a 2018 JAMA study found that people with below-average cardiorespiratory fitness for their age face double the all-cause mortality risk compared to those in the top quartile — a risk comparable to or exceeding smoking. A separate 2018 study found that maintaining five healthy lifestyle factors (non-smoking, healthy BMI, 30+ min moderate exercise daily, moderate alcohol, high diet quality) is associated with 12–14 extra years of healthy life. The compounding frame is the key insight: muscle mass begins declining in your 30s (sarcopenia), making early accumulation functionally equivalent to early retirement saving — the larger the "account," the longer you can draw it down before hitting frailty thresholds.

💡 Worth Reading

Forget the Good Life, Try the Good Day

Massimo Pigliucci (Stoic philosopher, author of How to Be a Stoic) argues that "the good life" is an abstraction that can become an obstacle — a distant destination that lets you defer living well indefinitely. The Stoic alternative is to zoom in on the day as the only unit of life actually under your control, which is both more tractable and more honest about the nature of time. This is a clean, practical restatement of the Meditations' emphasis on the present moment — and a useful corrective to the "I'll be happy when..." structure most people operate on.

Figs in Winter (Pigliucci)

The Stoic Taxonomy of Anger

Donald Robertson breaks down how the Stoics (particularly Seneca in De Ira) classified anger not as a single emotion but as a multi-stage process: a pre-emotional impression, a judgment that harm was done unjustly, and then the passionate response. The practical implication is that intervention is possible at each stage — the Stoics weren't advising suppression but rather catching the automatic appraisal before it becomes a full emotional commitment. This is directly applicable to any high-pressure decision-making context where reactive anger distorts judgment.

Donald Robertson — The Stoic Mind

How to Mentally Rehearse Coping

The second lesson in Robertson's Stoic Mind series focuses on premeditatio malorum — the Stoic practice of pre-visualizing adversity — but operationalized as a specific mental rehearsal technique rather than vague worry. The distinction Robertson draws is between catastrophizing (passive, emotional) and structured coping rehearsal (active, rational): you imagine the difficulty AND your competent response to it. This is the same technique now used in cognitive behavioral therapy and sports psychology, with a 2,000-year precedent.

Donald Robertson — The Stoic Mind

Leo XIV's Encyclical on AI — A Secular-Stoic Analysis

Pigliucci reads the new Pope's formal document on AI ethics through a Stoic lens, asking where the Catholic framework and Stoic ethics converge or diverge on questions of human dignity, technological agency, and what makes a life worth living. Worth reading less for the theology and more for Pigliucci using it as a forcing function to articulate what a Stoic ethics of AI would actually look like — which turns out to center on: does this technology support or undermine rationality and the capacity for virtue?

Figs in Winter (Pigliucci)
