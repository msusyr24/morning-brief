Jared Alderman

Jared makes a sharp point about the psychology of passive lines: players conflate aggression with agency and assume checking signals weakness or lack of control, when strategically checking is identical in weight to betting — it's just a different decision within the rules. The real trap he identifies is players paying a "betting premium" (like insurance) to end hands early rather than letting passive lines extract more value from opponents who will over-bluff or over-call in spots where a check-call is superior. He also warns against collapsing opponent reads into two-dimensional archetypes ("station," "aggro") when most players have very specific spots where they never bluff and others where they always over-bluff — and exploiting them requires mapping those contours precisely.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia: Why Keto Works (And It's Not Insulin)

Attia's clearest statement yet on his view change: ketogenic diets work because they make caloric restriction easier and improve satiety — not because they suppress insulin in some special way. The falsifier is simple: you can gain weight in ketosis if you eat a surplus. He's now agnostic on dietary tactics as long as patients hit ≥1.4g/kg protein. The practical upshot: the mechanism debate (insulin vs. energy balance) matters less than adherence and protein adequacy, and anyone who held strong opinions about why low-carb works should update accordingly.

On fasting: after three years of quarterly 7–10 day fasts, he scaled back significantly. His core concern is that human autophagy data is thin — there's no clean measurement instrument, and the "3 days to significant autophagy" signal came from expert consensus and preclinical data, not human RCTs. The evidence-to-personal-cost ratio didn't hold up over time.

Dr. Sten Ekberg: The 2018 Fructose Mechanism Update

The prevailing model for 17 years (Robert Lustig's "fructose goes straight to liver, only liver processes it") was corrected by a 2018 finding: the small intestine processes fructose first and clears ~90% at low doses, with the liver acting as overflow. The critical variable is dose and speed: liquid fructose on an empty stomach saturates gut capacity fastest (threshold estimated ~10–25g/sitting in humans, still uncertain). Practical benchmarks: a can of Coke (~21g fructose) clearly overwhelms gut capacity; berries (~2.5–5g per serving) likely don't. Fructose is still harmful — separately, it fails to suppress ghrelin, doesn't trigger insulin-mediated satiety, and activates brain reward circuits more than glucose — but the mechanism is more nuanced than "liver poison."

Peter Attia: Gray-Market Peptides and Big Pharma's Fingerprints

Attia's team traced the patent and development histories of 25 popular gray-market peptides (the kinds sold as "research chemicals") and found deep ties to pharmaceutical R&D — many were originally developed by or licensed from major pharma companies before being abandoned for commercial reasons and migrating to the gray market. The implication is that the "underground biohacker vs. evil pharma" framing around peptides is largely false; many of these compounds have surprisingly rigorous preclinical histories, which is both reassuring about mechanisms and concerning about the purity and dosing of what people are actually buying.

Peter Attia MD

Walter Longo / Thomas DeLauer: Fasting-Mimicking Diet Resets Metabolic Damage in Mice

Middle-aged mice put on a western diet (high-fat, high-sugar) who also received 5 days/month of fasting-mimicking diet (FMD) showed complete restoration of metabolic health and longevity equivalent to a healthy-diet control group — not a partial improvement. Longo's hypothesis: bad food may not cause incremental daily damage but rather cumulatively tips a biological "switch," and FMD periodically prevents that switch from flipping. Key mechanistic candidate: reducing growth hormone / IGF-1 signaling, which Longo identifies as the master regulator blocking autophagy, stem cell activation, and metabolic reprogramming. A 500-person Italian trial testing quarterly (every 3 months) FMD is complete but results aren't yet published.

💡 Worth Reading

16 AI Agents Given Bitcoin — Wealth Concentration Emerged Spontaneously

Tyler Cowen flags an experiment: 16 AI agents were each given 1,000 satoshis of real Bitcoin and no instructions on how to use it. After 100 rounds of whatever they chose to do, four agents had roughly doubled their holdings, two were wiped out, and the three wealthiest held more than half of all the Bitcoin. No rules imposed this outcome — it emerged from agent behavior. The implied parallel to human economic outcomes and Pareto distributions is uncomfortable and worth sitting with.

Bleached Corals Recover Within Days by Touching Healthy Neighbors

A Nature-published finding that bleached corals can recover their symbiotic algae within days simply through physical contact with healthy neighboring corals — a mechanism no one had documented before. The implication for reef restoration is significant: passive recovery through proximity may be far more powerful than assumed, and restoration strategies that focus only on transplanting healthy corals may be missing the value of maintaining physical contact networks within reefs.

Stoic Premeditation of Anger — Donald Robertson

Robertson unpacks the Stoic technique of premeditation specifically applied to anger — not just the standard premeditatio malorum (imagining future adversity), but the therapeutic practice of anticipating your own anger triggers before they occur and pre-deciding your response. The key Stoic move: anger arises from an initial "impression" that a wrong has been done, and you can disrupt it before it escalates if you've pre-examined whether the impression is accurate. Pairs well with Marcus Aurelius on how the first moment of pause is where the entire battle is won or lost.
