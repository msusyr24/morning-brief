Chesterton's Fence

Before removing a rule or system you don't understand, figure out why it was put there. Every rule was someone's solution to a problem. If you can't articulate the problem, you might be about to recreate it. Munger applies this to business reorganizations: understand the old structure first.

Jared Alderman

Jared makes a crisp argument against the reflex toward aggression in poker: checking is not passivity, it's a decision equally within the rules, and the psychological bias toward "taking control" by betting often manifests as expensive insurance-buying — paying small amounts to end hands that would be more profitable to play through. His broader point is that opponent modeling needs to be multi-dimensional even for weak players; a player who check-raises top pair for value but never bluffs isn't simply "aggressive" — they have a specific, exploitable shape, and collapsing them to a one-word archetype prevents you from finding the right response.

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz covers a new Nature study on bowhead whales — which live 200+ years and have 1,000x more cells than humans, yet get far less cancer — and the counterintuitive finding: whale cells actually become cancerous after fewer mutations than human cells, not more. The answer lies not in eliminating damaged cells but in repairing them faster. The key protein is CIRBP (cold-inducible RNA binding protein), which whales produce in much higher quantities; when flies were genetically engineered to produce it, they survived X-ray radiation damage at twice the rate of controls. Critically, humans already produce CIRBP — just less of it — meaning this isn't science fiction requiring wholly foreign machinery. He also covers the thymus and immune surveillance: new 2026 research shows adults with healthier thymic function develop cancer at measurably lower rates, and researchers are exploring growth hormone peptides (tesamorelin, CJC) to rejuvenate thymic function, though he's careful to note this isn't proven cancer prevention.

Dr. Sten Ekberg

Ekberg revisits Dr. Robert Lustig's famous fructose lecture (25M views, 17 years of influence) to flag a significant correction from a 2018 mouse study: fructose is not primarily metabolized by the liver first — it's cleared by the small intestine first, with the liver acting as overflow handler. At low doses (~10–25g of fructose per sitting in humans, though exact amounts uncertain), the gut handles ~90% of fructose; only the excess reaches the liver and gets converted to fat. This makes fructose metabolism dose-dependent and speed-dependent: liquid sugar on an empty stomach overwhelms the gut fast; fiber-buffered fruit eaten slowly may stay well under the threshold. Practical upshots: a can of Coke (21g fructose), apple juice (14g/8oz), and a medium fast-food milkshake (25g) all likely overflow to the liver in typical consumption conditions; whole berries and citrus probably don't. Fructose's other harms — blunted ghrelin response, heightened reward signaling, no insulin-mediated satiety — remain intact regardless.

🧬 Health & Science

Bowhead Whale CIRBP Study (via Nick Norwitz)

A new Nature paper identified CIRBP (cold-inducible RNA binding protein) as a key driver of bowhead whale longevity and cancer resistance. The paradox: whale cells become cancerous after fewer mutations than human cells — yet whales rarely get cancer. The mechanism is superior DNA repair, not cellular resistance to mutation initiation. When flies were engineered to express whale CIRBP, they survived X-ray-induced DNA damage at twice the rate of controls; human CIRBP (a lower-expression version of the same protein) also extended fly lifespan when expressed in higher quantities. The authors explicitly state: "DNA repair will be difficult or impossible to improve in humans" is incorrect — the bowhead whale disproves this. Gene editing and tissue-specific protein upregulation are identified as plausible intervention paths, and CRISPR applications for proteins like this are already in human clinical use (e.g., sickle cell).

Via Nick Norwitz

Epitgram (Myostatin Inhibitor) — Missing Piece for GLP-1 Users

A new monoclonal antibody called epitgram, which inhibits myostatin (a muscle-growth suppressor), preserved 4 lbs more lean mass vs. control in a 24-week trial alongside aggressive weight loss drugs — a 55% reduction in muscle loss — with zero resistance training. Subjects also lost ~1 lb more fat, plausibly because added muscle mass increases total energy expenditure. The framing from Renaissance Periodization: GLP-1 drugs are highly effective for fat loss but carry significant muscle-wasting as a side effect; epitgram may be the pharmacological patch for that problem. This is a new drug category in development, not yet widely available.

Via Renaissance Periodization

Fructose Metabolism Correction: Liver Is Backup, Not First Stop

A 2018 study (confirmed by follow-up research) overturns the Lustig model: the small intestine clears roughly 90% of fructose at low doses, converting it to glucose and lactate; the liver only handles the overflow. This makes harm dose- and speed-dependent — liquid sugar on an empty stomach saturates gut capacity fast, flooding the liver; fiber-buffered fruit eaten slowly may stay below the threshold (~10–25g fructose per sitting estimated for humans, though not precisely established). Practical benchmarks from Ekberg: a 20oz soda delivers ~36g fructose (well over threshold); OJ is right at the edge at 10g/8oz; whole berries and citrus (2–5g/100g) likely stay under. The other harms of fructose — no ghrelin suppression, elevated reward signaling, no insulin-mediated satiety — are not affected by this revision.

Via Dr. Sten Ekberg

Cancer: What's Changed in 15 Years (Attia × Mukherjee)

Key clinical updates from the conversation: colorectal cancer rising in young people is genuinely unexplained; breast cancer mortality gains have plateaued after two decades of improvement; liquid biopsies (ctDNA tests) appear to generate net harm in unselected populations via false positives but may be useful in high-risk genetic carriers. On treatment: immunotherapy (anti-PD-1/PD-L1) now meaningfully extends survival in lung cancer; CAR-T has produced 15-year survivors in previously untreatable leukemias; new small molecules like sotorasib (KRAS inhibitor) are producing months-to-years of extended survival in pancreatic cancer that spreads — previously near-impossible. On hereditary risk: "shove" mutations (BRCA1/2) are well-understood; the growing frontier is "nudge" mutations — hundreds of low-effect polymorphisms that cumulatively elevate risk significantly and are now detectable via polygenic risk scoring.

Stoic Premeditation of Anger

Donald Robertson covers the Stoic practice of premeditation applied specifically to anger — treating it as a preventative therapy rather than a reactive one. The Stoics (particularly Seneca) argued that anger, unlike most emotions, has a narrow window for interception before it becomes self-reinforcing; the practice involves mentally rehearsing provocations in advance so that when they arrive, you've already chosen your response. Pairs well with the "closed loop" mental frameworks discussed in the Rogan episode.

On the Indispensability of Logic — Epictetus, Discourses 1.17

Massimo Pigliucci unpacks Epictetus's argument that logic is not a mere intellectual game but the foundational tool for living well — you cannot assess what is truly good for you without the ability to reason clearly, and a person who cannot reason cannot be truly free. Epictetus is sharper here than Marcus or Seneca: he argues that even identifying which impressions to assent to requires a trained faculty of judgment, making logic a prerequisite for virtue rather than just an ornament of education.
