Jared Alderman ft. Uri Peleg

This is a genuinely interesting theory conversation about hidden information as a strategic resource in poker. Uri's core point: GTO solvers assume both players know each other's game tree, but in practice your opponent doesn't know your strategy — which means you can deviate from pure balance in specific spots (e.g., over-bluffing ace-high boards, under-bluffing king-high boards) and it's practically undetectable. His concrete claim: "It only matters if I bluff too much or too little if my opponent calls too much or too little." He also makes a sharp point about randomizing bet sizing — the theoretical case that mixing sizes across spots changes what a check communicates, potentially creating exploitable hidden information about where you are in the game tree. The chessboard-as-range analogy (your range is your invisible board; GTO is setting it up optimally not knowing your opponent's) is the cleanest framing of the concept I've heard.

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz covers a new paper on engineered probiotic bacteria (E. coli Nissle 1917, modified with a glucose-sensing system called HexR) that detects dietary carbohydrates and releases GLP-1 in response — essentially a living drug that mimics Ozempic's mechanism but is triggered by food intake rather than a constant injection. In diabetic mice over 30 days: decreased food intake, weight gain, fat mass, inflammation, triglycerides, fasting glucose, insulin resistance, and a lower respiratory exchange ratio (more fat burning). In monkeys: a single dose raised GLP-1 within 2 hours and reduced blood sugar for 3 days; 5-week treatment restored healthier metabolic markers with no notable side effects. The most interesting tangent: a 2026 PNAS paper found GLP-1 drugs activate hunger neurons in the brain that increase fat utilization — and when those neurons are disrupted, weight returns even with appetite still suppressed. This suggests "step two" of GLP-1 efficacy is metabolic flexibility, not just appetite, which is why keto/fasting may help maintain weight loss post-GLP-1 discontinuation. He also flags a controlled trial showing statin treatment (particularly atorvastatin) reduced GLP-1 levels by ~90% in subjects, a finding he says none of the cardiologists he questioned at Harvard-affiliated hospitals had heard of.

Peter Attia ft. Jeff Cavaliere

Cavaliere (physical therapist, former NY Mets strength coach) argues that injury is never a mandate to rest completely — it's a mandate to find better-loaded, better-progressed exercise alternatives. His own training history illustrates this: he has a 50% labral tear plus partial supraspinatus tear in one shoulder and severe flat feet that have generated cascading knee and hip degeneration, yet he trains around all of it. The core rehab-to-performance philosophy: the same flexibility required to adapt a protocol for a post-op patient applies equally to strength training — there's never only one exercise, and if an exercise doesn't hit the target muscle or aggravates a joint, you find another. He pushes back on the trend toward short-form fitness content: the "sets and reps please" mentality strips the "why," making knowledge non-transferable. The broader clinical point is that most training injuries in his own case trace back to overzealous early training without correcting structural issues (flat feet, in his case) that were detectable and addressable at the time.

Lex Fridman ft. Mathematicians (Brunton, Moitra, Alex)

This is a substantive emergency roundtable on OpenAI releasing proofs of several major mathematical conjectures — quasi-Riemann Hypothesis, Navier-Stokes existence/uniqueness, Unique Games Conjecture, 3SUM improvements, and hundreds more. The mathematicians' honest assessment: the proofs appear to be within the "semigroup generated by known moves" (their preferred framing over the dismissive "convex hull") — meaning AI assembled existing techniques in long, non-obvious cascades rather than introducing genuinely new mathematical ideas or definitions. But the semigroup's reach is far larger than any human could traverse, which is the actual news. Terence Tao's concern (cited directly): the release "casually disrupts or destroys" years of grad student and postdoc work on motivating problems, because knowing a solution exists changes how the problem can be explored. The panelists' most interesting reframe is the "doorman fallacy" — reducing a mathematician's job to "proves theorems" misses what they actually do: ask the right questions, build theory structures that solve surrounding problems, create generalizable abstractions. The consensus is that asking good questions and developing new definitions remains a genuinely human contribution that AI hasn't yet replicated — but the panelists acknowledge this boundary may not hold. The factoring/P=NP point is noted: if AI solved those, it would immediately break global e-commerce encryption, which is why certain results would go through governments before public release.

Modern Wisdom ft. Joe Hudson

Hudson's framework for decision-making is grounded in neuroscience (citing Damasio): the emotional center of the brain makes decisions, full stop — logic is only used to model which emotional state a given choice will produce. The practical implication is that indecisiveness is always an emotional problem, never an informational one: fear of a specific feeling (rejection, failure, looking like a dweeb) literally removes options from your decision space because you can't see past the feared outcome. His distinction between "choices" (automatic, subconscious) and "decisions" (deliberate) is useful: the goal of training is to push more decisions into the choice category, reducing cognitive load. Fear also causes binary thinking (should I/shouldn't I) and a "false end" illusion — you can't see past the decision to the next decision and the one after. His reframe for his barber: offered $2,000 to collect 50 rejections in 60 days, the barber couldn't do it — he got 2 acceptances and now sells his art professionally. The Modern Wisdom short (envy/sacrifices) pairs well: "You only envy the lives of people whose sacrifices you can't see" connects directly to the emotional distortion that drives poor decisions.

Greene's new book The Law of the Sublime is about expanding your experiential consciousness — moving outside the "circle" of familiar, habitual perception. His argument is that childhood consciousness is naturally wide (everything is novel, imagination animates everything), and it progressively narrows through socialization, role-assignment, and routine. The exercises he developed for readers are designed to practice this perceptual shift without requiring exotic experience or drugs.

🧬 Health & Science

GLP-1 Mechanism Update: The "Step Two" Fat-Burning Effect

Nick Norwitz covers a 2026 PNAS paper finding that GLP-1 drugs activate hunger neurons in the brain that — paradoxically — increase fat utilization and metabolic flexibility rather than appetite. When researchers disrupted these neurons in animal models, weight rebounded aggressively even though appetite suppression remained intact, suggesting that metabolic flexibility (fat burning) is what sustains GLP-1 weight loss, not just caloric restriction. The practical implication: roughly 50% of people who stop GLP-1 medications within a year may benefit from ketogenic or fasting-based approaches afterward, since these partially replicate the metabolic adaptation GLP-1 creates. Early literature cited suggests keto-adapted patients can discontinue GLP-1s without weight regain. This reframes GLP-1s as two-mechanism drugs: step one is appetite suppression, step two is fat-burning mode — and losing step two is what causes rebound.

Source: Nick Norwitz — Could This Probiotic Make Ozempic Obsolete?

Statins and GLP-1 Suppression

Norwitz highlights a small controlled trial published in Cell Metabolism finding that atorvastatin treatment dramatically reduced GLP-1 levels — the graph he shows depicts a ~90% drop in GLP-1 response. He polled dozens of cardiologists at Harvard-affiliated hospitals and found none had encountered this study. The finding is worth flagging not as a reason to stop statins, but as an example of a clinically relevant interaction that appears absent from mainstream cardiology practice awareness — particularly relevant given simultaneous societal interest in GLP-1 therapies for metabolic health.

Restless Legs Syndrome: Iron Delivery and Blood Sugar, Not Just Dopamine

Thomas DeLauer summarizes research showing RLS is primarily a brain iron delivery failure (low ferritin in cerebrospinal fluid even when blood iron is "normal"), not a simple dopamine shortage. A neurology study measured oxygen pressure through leg skin during RLS immobilization tests and found a >20% oxygen drop in the legs (not chest), which normalized when dopamine compounds were administered — suggesting the urge to move is a genuine oxygen-seeking response, not a neurological misfire. The dopamine connection is more nuanced than typical treatment suggests: tyrrosine hydroxylase (the enzyme that makes dopamine) was elevated in RLS brain tissue, while D2 receptors were downregulated — meaning more dopamine is being produced but fewer receptors are catching it. Standard dopamine agonist drugs cause "augmentation" (symptoms worsen over months) because they add more dopamine to a system already overproducing. A meta-analysis from Baylor (12 RCTs, 500 people) found iron supplementation significantly improved RLS, with the threshold being ferritin below 75 µg/L — well above the standard clinical "deficiency" cutoff of 30, meaning most patients are told their iron is fine when it isn't sufficient for brain signaling.

Source: Thomas DeLauer — Restless Legs is a Warning Sign

Peter Attia / Jeff Cavaliere: Training Around Injury and Structural Issues

Cavaliere's primary clinical argument: pain or injury is a signal to load more appropriately, not to stop training. He illustrates with his own case — 50% labral tear, partial supraspinatus tear, severe flat feet causing cascading degeneration — and maintains near-full training by continually adjusting exercise selection rather than resting. The physical therapy principle he applies: the same diagnostic flexibility you'd use post-surgery (adapting when the protocol doesn't match what the patient can actually do) should govern training program design. The structural insight on flat feet is actionable: poor arch mechanics generate cumulative stress upstream (knee, hip, low back) over thousands of repetitions, and ignoring correctable structural issues at 20 compounds significantly by 50.
