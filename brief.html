GTO Lab — Nick Petrangelo, Daniel Dvoress & Axel Hallay

Excellent deep-dive on mystery bounty tournaments as a structurally different game from vanilla MTTs. The core insight: most players massively underadjust — not just "play a little looser" but recognize that at the bounty-kick-in stage with large fields, ICM is essentially irrelevant, chips are worth the least relative to the prize pool, and VPIP from early positions can approach 100% in certain spots. They walk through their library of ~300 sims built around the KO-to-stack ratio (bounty value ÷ current stack), which is the governing variable for all pre- and post-flop strategy. The EV breakdown tool separating regular prize pool EV (green) from KO pool EV (yellow) is the clearest visualization of the core trade-off: some hands are pure yellow — positive EV only because of the bounty, with zero expected prize pool value — and most players fold these, leaving money on the table. Key takeaway: manage the bounty-vs.-tournament trade-off explicitly at every stage; the final table is a completely different calculus from the first day of a 1,000-person field.

Jared Alderman — with Uri Peleg

Dense theory conversation about hidden information as an underexplored edge. Uri's sharpest point: balance only matters if your opponent can detect and exploit your imbalances. If you over-bluff ace-high boards and under-bluff king-high boards but are globally near-correct in frequency, there is no practical way for opponents to discover this — meaning you have more freedom than GTO orthodoxy implies. The chess/chessboard analogy is clarifying: GTO is setting up an invisible board where you know your own positions but not your opponent's; exploitative play is guessing their setup and countering. Uri also makes the point that at even nosebleed stakes, almost no players have the full game tree mapped for a single-size strategy — so the "correct" play is often less about precision and more about not being face-up. His practical heuristic for profiling: check back certain hands specifically to gather information about whether opponents are too aggro or too passive on later streets.

Renaissance Periodization — Dr. Mike

Summary of a massive umbrella review (137 systematic reviews, ~30,000 participants) on resistance training. Five findings worth locking in: (1) Volume is the primary driver of hypertrophy — more than 10 sets/week per muscle beats fewer, with meaningful gains extending past 30 sets/week, though with strong diminishing returns above 20. (2) Training close to failure matters — stopping 4+ reps shy produces notably worse gains; stopping 1-2 reps shy is essentially equivalent to true failure. (3) Rep range is largely irrelevant — 5 to ~30 reps produces roughly equivalent hypertrophy if effort is equated; below 5 or above 30 is suboptimal. (4) Frequency per se doesn't drive gains — volume and quality do, but higher frequency enables more volume and better quality per set (fatigue dilutes stimulus after ~15 sets/session). (5) Rep tempo, exercise order, and advanced periodization nuances show barely detectable effects in the literature — the basics dominate almost everything. For strength specifically: twice-weekly beats once-weekly due to nervous system practice, and earlier exercises in a session get the best strength adaptations.

Peter Attia — Jeff Cavaliere

Cavaliere's framework for reps in reserve (RIR) is practical and well-articulated. Zero RIR = mechanical failure (can't complete another rep in good form); 1 RIR = one grinding rep left; 2-3 RIR = still challenging but no visible effort to outside observer; 4+ RIR = junk volume. His key claim: most people underestimate their true capacity by 1-3 reps, so when they think they're at 2 RIR they're often at 5+. His recommendation: start by training to true mechanical failure frequently so you can calibrate the scale — without that anchor, everything else is miscalibrated. Distinguishes psychological failure (discomfort avoidance) from mechanical failure, and notes form failure (slower reps, slight loosening) is acceptable while joint pain is not. The practical upshot: by pushing to genuine form failure more often, you can reduce total set count while actually increasing the productive stimulus per session.

Lex Fridman — AI & Mathematics Crisis Episode

Genuine emergency episode with three mathematicians (Brunton, Moitra, and a number theorist) reacting to OpenAI dropping solutions to Navier-Stokes, a quasi-Riemann Hypothesis result, the Unique Games Conjecture, and hundreds of other results simultaneously. The honest tension: nobody has a clear answer for what the grad student career path looks like now, and the blueprint of "solve a problem people care about, get known" has been disrupted.

ModernWisdom — Joe Hudson

Joe Hudson on decision-making has a few genuinely sharp observations worth extracting. First: a terrible decision isn't one where you don't get what you want — it's one where you compromise yourself to get it, and there's something in you that always knows when that's happening. The muscle for detecting self-abandonment atrophies if ignored; resentment is the diagnostic signal (you resent others when you've abandoned yourself to accommodate them). Second: in business contexts, making 100 decisions a day and being wrong beats making 10 decisions carefully — because decisions are experiments, and experiments generate learning that compounds. The identity point ties it together: indecisiveness is usually identity under threat ("I have to be right because being wrong meant punishment"). People with stable identity are both more decisive and more open to being wrong — not in tension, because their identity isn't staked on the outcome. The paradox axes framing (high conviction AND high openness simultaneously) is worth sitting with.

🧬 Health & Science

Brown Fat, Metabolic Disease, and Activation Strategies

Nick Norwitz summarized a large Nature study (~100,000 participants) finding that individuals with detectable brown adipose tissue had 22-48% lower odds of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, depression, and anxiety. Brown fat is mitochondria-dense and thermogenic — it burns calories rather than storing them, which is the mechanistic link to metabolic health. One counterintuitive finding from the study: people with more brown fat had higher LDL cholesterol, which is a useful data point in the LDL-as-proxy-for-cardiovascular-risk debate. The study identified losartan (an ARB blood pressure medication) as an activator, alongside other dietary and environmental strategies including one dietary intervention claimed to increase energy expenditure by ~600 kcal/day in RCTs (not specified in the short — worth following up on the full masterclass).

Resistance Training Science: What 30,000 Participants Actually Show

The Renaissance Periodization umbrella review (137 systematic reviews, ~30,000 participants, PMID 41843416) is the strongest single data point on training in the brief. Key findings that contradict common gym wisdom: rep range between 5-30 reps produces roughly equal hypertrophy when effort is equated — so there is no meaningful argument for staying exclusively in an 8-12 rep range; stopping 4+ reps from failure produces significantly worse outcomes than stopping 1-2 reps shy; and frequency itself doesn't drive hypertrophy — volume and quality do, but higher frequency enables more of both. The practical upshot for programming: prioritize proximity to failure and weekly volume over any other variable; rep tempo, exercise order, and periodization schemes show barely detectable effects in the literature.

Video: 30,000 People Studied. This Is How You Should Train.

Reps in Reserve Calibration — Jeff Cavaliere on Peter Attia

Cavaliere's key empirical claim: most lifters systematically underestimate their true capacity by 1-3 reps, meaning what feels like 2 RIR is often 4-5 RIR. This matters because junk volume (sets done far from failure) accumulates wear without stimulus. His recommended fix: periodically train to true mechanical failure (not psychological failure, not form breakdown involving joint pain) so you can anchor the RIR scale accurately. Once calibrated, you can legitimately reduce total set count while increasing actual training stimulus — fewer but harder sets. The injury management corollary: an injury is not a mandate to rest, it's a mandate to find safer exercise variations and appropriate loads — a useful reframe for anyone managing chronic joint issues.
