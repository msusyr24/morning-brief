Nick Norwitz

Norwitz explains a mechanistic distinction most nutritionists miss: fructose from whole blueberries is processed in the small intestine, which converts much of it to organic acids and glucose before it reaches the liver — essentially shielding the liver. With syrup (or any fast-delivery liquid fructose), the dose overwhelms the intestine's capacity and the fructose hits the liver directly, driving de novo lipogenesis and contributing to fatty liver. This maps directly onto Dr. Ekberg's longer video below, which adds important nuance: the intestine clears ~90% of fructose at low doses but saturates quickly (possibly around 10–25g per sitting in humans), making dose and delivery speed the key variables.

🧬 Health & Science

Fructose Processing: The Lustig Model Needed an Update

A 2018 study (confirmed by follow-up research) overturned the dominant model: the small intestine, not the liver, processes fructose first, converting most of it to organic acids and glucose at low doses. The liver is a backup that only sees overflow. The critical implication is that this is highly dose- and speed-dependent: liquid fructose on an empty stomach saturates intestinal capacity fast (possibly around 10g), while whole fruit with fiber slows delivery enough that the intestine handles most of it. Practical hierarchy of risk: soda/juice/syrup > raisins/dates > whole low-fructose fruit (berries, citrus). Fructose is still harmful — it doesn't trigger ghrelin suppression or meaningful insulin response, meaning no satiety brake — but the mechanism is more nuanced than "liver gets overwhelmed by any fructose."

Dr. Sten Ekberg · Nick Norwitz

Insulin Resistance Has Visible Hand Markers — With Specific Mechanisms

Thomas DeLauer's video is more clinically grounded than most: curling fingers (Dupuytren's contracture) have a 3x odds ratio with insulin resistance and diabetes — the mechanism is advanced glycation end products (AGEs) cross-linking and stiffening collagen in the palmar fascia, not alcohol. Red palms track liver dysfunction via estradiol accumulation (the liver can't clear estrogen, estradiol ramps up nitric oxide, dilating palmar capillaries) and appeared in 23% of liver disease patients in a clinical dermatology review, scaling with severity. Terry's nails (opaque whitening, loss of the lunula) had a 5.7x odds ratio for cirrhosis and appear to be the same nitric oxide/small vessel dilation phenomenon showing up under the nail bed. Actionable: if you see persistent red palms, check estradiol, ALT, and AST together.

Thomas DeLauer

Food Processing Influences Metabolism and Brain Activity (Virginia Tech)

A Virginia Tech study found that ultra-processed foods influence metabolism and brain activity — specifically implicating effects on hutelin (a gut hormone) — adding mechanistic detail to the epidemiological case against UPF. Still early, but it's pointing toward neurological appetite dysregulation as the mechanism, consistent with the GLP-1/hypothalamus framing Saladino and others use.

Stoic Cost-Benefit Analysis

Donald Robertson's commentary on how the Stoics — particularly Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus — evaluated actions not just by outcome but by what performing them costs your character. The frame is: every action is also a practice of virtue or vice, and that second-order effect compounds. A useful counterweight to pure consequentialism for decision-making.

Donald Robertson / Substack

Stoicism and Wealth

Massimo Pigliucci addresses a real tension: Stoicism treats externals like wealth as "indifferents," yet many Stoics (Seneca prominently) were extremely wealthy. His resolution: the Stoic position isn't that wealth is bad, but that it must not be the source of your identity or the condition for your equanimity. You should be able to lose it without losing yourself — but acquiring and enjoying it isn't a contradiction.
