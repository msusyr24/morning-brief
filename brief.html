Jared Alderman

Alderman makes a sharp point about the psychological bias toward aggression in poker: players conflate "taking action" with agency, when checking is an equally deliberate strategic decision. His argument is that betting small to "end the hand now" is actually a form of insecurity — you're paying a premium to avoid uncertainty rather than maximizing EV. Against recreational players especially, the trap is creating a two-dimensional player model ("he's a station," "he's aggressive") that misses the specific situations where they always bluff vs. never bluff, which is where real exploitative edges live.

Peter Attia (with Siddhartha Mukherjee)

Mukherjee organizes the cancer landscape into three domains — prevention, early detection, treatment — and flags what's actually changed in 15 years. On prevention: we now know inflammatory states are likely pro-carcinogenic, but we lack a biomarker equivalent to "high cholesterol" to identify who's at risk. On genetics: the old BRCA1/2 story is incomplete — Mukherjee distinguishes "shove mutations" (single genes like BRCA1 that multiply risk 9-10x) from "nudge mutations" (hundreds of genes each adding small risk), and the latter category now explains a meaningful fraction of hereditary cancers we previously couldn't account for. On treatment: CAR-T cell therapy is producing 15-20 year survivors in some leukemias and lymphomas (not just extending survival by months), immunotherapy now meaningfully extends life for lung cancer in smokers, and metabolic approaches to cancer treatment are gaining traction as a second/third-line option. Colorectal cancer rising in young people remains a genuine mystery.

Nick Norwitz

Cooling cooked potatoes causes starch retrogradation — the starch chains reorganize into crystalline structures that digestive enzymes can't easily access, turning them into resistant starch that reaches the colon where gut bacteria ferment it into beneficial metabolites. In a calorie-controlled human trial, 40g/day of resistant starch significantly reduced visceral fat. The practical implication: eating leftover cold potatoes (or reheated ones, which retain some resistant starch) is metabolically different from eating them hot.

Thomas DeLauer

DeLauer covers a dose-response study (Journal of Nutrients) in which overweight adults ate carb-matched meals with either no avocado, half an avocado, or a full avocado. Both avocado doses produced significantly lower glucose and insulin over 6 hours — and critically, the half-avocado worked as well as the full avocado, pointing to a specific compound rather than simple calorie displacement. That compound is manoheptulose, a rare sugar in avocados that inhibits a liver enzyme (glucokinase) slowing hepatic glucose processing — a second mechanism distinct from fat/fiber slowing gastric emptying. A third route: a 12-week RCT found daily avocado shifted gut bacteria (notably Lachnospira, which produces butyrate) and was linked to lower post-meal insulin. Practical guidance: a quarter to half an avocado alongside your highest-carb meal is the target dose; you're not after the fat effect, you're after the manoheptulose.

Dr. Sten Ekberg

Ekberg argues the central mechanism in Robert Lustig's famous 25M-view fructose lecture — that fructose goes straight to the liver and is uniquely hepatotoxic — was factually wrong based on a 2018 discovery: at low doses (roughly 10-25g per sitting in humans), the small intestine actually processes ~90% of fructose first, converting it to glucose and lactate, with the liver only handling the overflow. This makes fructose harm dose- and speed-dependent: liquid fructose on an empty stomach overwhelms the gut quickly and sends overflow to the liver; fructose in whole fruit with fiber is buffered enough that little reaches the liver. The practical hierarchy: a single can of Coke has ~21g fructose (well above threshold), orange juice is at the cusp (~10g per 8oz), and most whole fruits are fine except medjool dates (32g per 100g). Fructose is still harmful, but for nuanced reasons — primarily its failure to suppress ghrelin and its amplification of food cravings, not solely the liver-overload mechanism.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia + Mukherjee: Cancer Prevention Biomarkers & the "Nudge vs. Shove" Mutation Model

The most actionable framework from this conversation: Mukherjee distinguishes "shove" mutations (BRCA1/2, P53 — single genes that multiply cancer risk 9-10x and are well-characterized) from "nudge" mutations — hundreds of genes each adding small increments of risk that, in aggregate, explain a substantial fraction of hereditary cancers we previously couldn't account for. The implication is that polygenic risk scoring for cancer (analogous to cardiovascular polygenic risk scores) is coming and will meaningfully change who gets screened more aggressively. On the prevention side: we have strong evidence that chronic inflammatory states are pro-carcinogenic, but still lack a reliable biomarker to identify individuals in those states — Mukherjee's phrase was "I don't have a high cholesterol for cancer yet." Vogelstein's finding that ~66% of cancer mutations are random (not hereditary or environmental) remains directionally accurate but the hereditary fraction is likely higher than the original 5% estimate as polygenic contributions are better understood.

Fructose Mechanism Update: Small Intestine Processes First, Liver is the Backup

A 2018 study (replicated in follow-up research) found that at low doses, ~90% of dietary fructose is cleared by the small intestine — not the liver — contradicting the mechanism cited by Lustig and repeated widely for 17 years. The liver only processes fructose when the gut's capacity is saturated, making harm dose- and delivery-speed-dependent. Estimated thresholds: ~10g fructose/sitting when consumed as liquid on an empty stomach; ~25g when consumed slowly with fiber. One 8oz can of Coke contains ~21g fructose; a cup of strawberries contains ~4g. The reason fructose is still harmful: it fails to suppress the hunger hormone ghrelin (unlike glucose or protein/fat), dramatically increases appetite and food reward salience, and the overflow mechanism to the liver still applies at typical modern consumption levels — the new finding just means total elimination of fructose isn't necessary at low, buffered doses.

Avocado's Glucose-Blunting Mechanisms: Three Distinct Pathways

The calorie-matched dose-response trial (Journal of Nutrients) showing half an avocado works as well as a full one to blunt 6-hour glucose and insulin response points to manoheptulose — a rare C7 sugar in avocados that inhibits liver glucokinase, slowing hepatic processing of incoming glucose — as the active compound beyond fat/fiber's gastric emptying effects. The gut microbiome pathway (12-week RCT, n=163) showed avocado increased Lachnospira (a butyrate-producing bacterium) by 26-65%, and higher Lachnospira correlated with lower post-meal insulin independent of the acute effect. Practical implication: ~quarter to half an avocado alongside high-carb meals, daily for cumulative microbiome effects.

💡 Worth Reading

Hybrid Reasoning Agents: LLM agents that think in latent space, act through text

This paper proposes a new architecture where AI agents perform their reasoning in continuous "latent space" (raw neural activations, not words) but only produce text when they need to act in the world. The significance: current chain-of-thought reasoning forces models to think in human-readable tokens, which may constrain the kinds of computations available to them — this approach decouples internal reasoning from linguistic output, potentially enabling qualitatively different (and more efficient) cognition. Still a research paper, but it points toward a near-future where AI "thinking" is not the same thing as AI "talking."
