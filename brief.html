GTO Wizard

Jared Alderman walks through a real final-table hand using GTO Wizard's new preflop ICM solver — a tool that lets you input exact stack sizes, payouts, and even opponent tendencies ("player profiles") to get a solution in seconds. The core finding: 4-bet jamming QJs over a chip leader's 3-bet was likely a punt, costing the player ~$60K relative to the next pay jump. The deeper lesson: when facing an aggressive chip leader, not opening in the first place is often better than opening with the intention of jamming over a squeeze. Node-locking and player profiles let you test whether exploitative reads justify deviating — and in this case, it would take an extreme assumption to make the jam correct.

Andrew Huberman (ft. Dr. Andy Galpin)

A dense essentials recap on strength and hypertrophy programming. Key data points: after 40, you lose ~1% muscle mass per year but 2-4% of strength and 8-10% of muscle power — meaning power loss is the primary functional threat of aging, not mass. Strength training is the only intervention that combats neuromuscular aging; even adults over 90 showed 30-170% improvements in muscle size in 12-week studies. On programming: the "3-5 protocol" (3-5 days, 3-5 exercises, 3-5 sets of 3-5 reps, 3-5 min rest) is a durable framework for strength; progressive overload of ~3-5% per week is the key driver. For hypertrophy, rep tempo matters less than for strength — any cadence works as long as volume accumulates. The distinction that matters: strength requires practicing fast, heavy movement (force = mass × acceleration), while hypertrophy just requires sufficient mechanical stimulus regardless of tempo.

Peter Attia

A thorough breakdown of why thyroid management is so contested and commonly mishandled. Attia's core framework: thyroid is a Goldilocks hormone system (too much and too little are both harmful), which makes it uniquely prone to both under- and over-treatment. The pituitary marker TSH tells you what the gland is being asked to do, not what's actually happening in tissue — because conversion of inactive T4 to active T3 happens locally, organ by organ, and is not visible from standard labs. He identifies two failure modes: (1) conventional medicine dismissing real dysfunction because TSH is technically "normal" while the patient has positive antibodies, patchy gland, and family history; (2) functional medicine overcorrecting by treating borderline reverse T3 or free T3 as irrefutable hypothyroidism regardless of TSH, building elaborate "root cause" narratives around findings that often normalize spontaneously. His prescription: a multi-data-source approach combining labs, symptoms, and context — neither lab-value-in-isolation nor symptom-first.

🧬 Health & Science

Thyroid Management: The Two Failure Modes (Peter Attia)

The most actionable clinical framework in this brief. Attia identifies why thyroid is chronically mismanaged: TSH measures pituitary signaling, not tissue-level T3 activity, because conversion happens locally organ by organ. The under-treatment failure: dismissing a patient with elevated TSH at 4.2, positive antibodies, and a patchy gland because the number is technically "normal." The over-treatment failure: functional medicine practitioners treating borderline reverse T3 as irrefutable hypothyroidism, prescribing T3/T4 replacement that can cause atrial fibrillation, bone loss, and cognitive symptoms in patients who would normalize spontaneously. The practical upshot: always pair labs with symptoms and context; mildly elevated TSH frequently self-resolves on repeat testing and should not auto-trigger treatment. Full episode here.

Luteolin: Preclinical Metabolic Data Worth Tracking

Thomas DeLauer's deep-dive covers a flavonoid (found in parsley, celery, thyme, artichoke) with a striking but heavily caveated rodent data profile: fasting insulin dropped ~70% over 28 days in a high-fat diet mouse model; fat cell hypertrophy was suppressed; LDL dropped 33% and total cholesterol 30% in an atherosclerosis model over 14 weeks; two separate Alzheimer's models showed structural hippocampal protection. The fat-loss mechanism appears serotonin-mediated (knocking out serotonin synthesis eliminated the effect), and the anti-inflammatory action is selective — it suppressed pro-inflammatory gene expression while maintaining anti-inflammatory markers, which is unusual. Critical caveat: all data is preclinical. Bioavailability is ~17% and improves with fat-containing meals. Drug interaction risk via CYP1A2/3A4 inhibition if on medications. Dose range in studies: 100-200mg/day. Full breakdown here.

Strength & Hypertrophy: The Power Loss Problem (Huberman / Galpin)

The most underappreciated aging data from the Galpin episode: muscle mass declines ~1%/year after 40, but strength declines 2-4%/year and muscle power declines 8-10%/year. Functional independence as you age is primarily a power problem, not a size problem — power is what lets you catch yourself from a fall or stand from a chair. The only intervention that addresses this is heavy overload resistance training. Even adults 90+ showed 30-170% improvements in muscle size and strength in 12-week training studies, meaning it is never too late to start. The 3-5 framework (days/exercises/sets/reps/rest minutes) is the cleanest entry-point protocol in the literature for simultaneous strength development.

Pancreatic Cancer Early Detection: The Insulin Signal

Dr. Sten Ekberg makes a genuinely useful clinical point: new-onset diabetes after age 50 that doesn't match typical type 2 risk factors — and crucially, where insulin levels are low rather than high — is a red flag for pancreatic cancer destroying beta cells. Most physicians default to a type 2 diagnosis without testing insulin levels, which would immediately reveal the mismatch. He argues routine insulin testing would catch this pattern early; the other early signs (early satiety, vague upper abdominal discomfort, mild intermittent nausea) are individually non-specific but cluster meaningfully when combined. Painless jaundice (yellowing of the sclera without pain, versus the acute pain of a gallstone blockage) is the key later-stage signal. Full video.

Why Is the Liver So Weirdly Regenerative?

A substantive explainer on one of biology's genuine puzzles: the liver can regrow to full size after losing up to 70% of its mass, something almost no other organ can do. The piece goes into the mechanisms — hepatocyte proliferation, signaling pathways, and why this capacity exists in the liver but not, say, the heart — and what it tells us about tissue regeneration more broadly. Worth reading if you're interested in longevity biology or just want a genuinely surprising fact explained rigorously.

The Past Is Not a Defendant (Massimo Pigliucci)

Pigliucci argues that retroactive moral judgment of historical figures and cultures is intellectually lazy and practically useless — what we should be doing is learning from them, understanding the conditions that produced their choices, and asking what those conditions tell us about the constraints and errors of our own time. A clean application of Stoic-adjacent historical thinking: focus on what is useful and actionable, not on performing condemnation of the dead.

How to Cope Wisely with Anxiety (Donald Robertson)

Part of Robertson's Stoic Mind series, this applies Stoic cognitive techniques specifically to anxiety — distinguishing between what is genuinely in our control (our judgments and responses) versus what produces the anxious response (external uncertainty). Robertson draws on the classical distinction between the impression and the assent to show how the Stoics would have approached what we now call cognitive reappraisal. Practical and grounded in the actual philosophical texts rather than pop-Stoicism.
