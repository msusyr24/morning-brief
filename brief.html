Jared Alderman

Jared works through a hand where a live player folds two pair on the turn to a min-click after bet-calling the flop — a spot theory says is worth ~$50 in EV to call. His broader point: live players make massive deviations and have no calibrated sense of how large the error is. They often think in binary ("I know he has a set, so I fold") rather than in frequencies and pot odds. He distinguishes between accurate read (yes, this player has a set a high % of the time) and correct action (you're still getting a price that demands a call at reasonable frequencies). The online-vs-live gap isn't reads — it's that online players have internalized that even likely-bad spots can be profitable calls because of price.

GTO Lab

Short but substantive: explains the "collision effect" in tournament ICM — when two or more shorter stacks are likely to clash against each other or the blinds, a player with a medium stack gains value passively just by surviving, which means their chip EV of calling or getting involved goes down even holding strong hands. The takeaway is that ICM pressure compounds from multiple directions simultaneously, not just from the pay jump.

Dr. Sten Ekberg

Ekberg argues that the famous Lustig "fructose goes straight to the liver" mechanism — cited by health professionals for 17 years — was overturned by a 2018 mouse study showing the small intestine processes fructose first, clearing ~90% of low doses before it reaches the liver. The liver is the backup, not the primary processor. The key dose-dependent threshold in humans is estimated at 10–25g of fructose per sitting; below that (especially with fiber, slower eating, or as part of a meal), the liver is largely spared. Above it — triggered quickly by liquid sugar, empty stomach, or high-fructose corn syrup — the overflow hits the liver and drives fat synthesis. Practical numbers: a regular soda has ~21g fructose, apple juice ~14g, a single Medjool date ~32g per 100g. Fructose also suppresses ghrelin (satiety hormone) far less than glucose or fat/protein, doesn't trigger a meaningful insulin response, and causes brain reward centers to light up more strongly — creating a feedback loop for overconsumption even when the liver is being partially spared.

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz flags a counterintuitive finding: GLP-1 drugs activate hunger neurons (AgRP neurons), yet still cause weight loss. The key is that these neurons have a dual function — new research shows GLP-1 causes them to shift fuel burning from carbohydrates toward fat (similar to a ketogenic metabolic state). When researchers chemically blocked these neurons in animals, initial weight loss occurred but body fat rebounded aggressively even with sustained calorie restriction — the hunger neurons' fat-burning function was lost. This suggests GLP-1's durability depends less on appetite suppression alone and more on a metabolic substrate shift.

Thomas DeLauer

DeLauer makes a detailed case for allulose as a metabolically active sweetener rather than a passive sugar substitute. Four human RCTs he cites: (1) 10g allulose alongside 50g sugar reduced blood glucose and insulin significantly within 30 minutes in a dose-dependent fashion; (2) in insulin-resistant subjects, 10g allulose reduced glucose AUC by 8% vs. fructose (no effect); (3) insulin was 33% lower when allulose was added to sucrose vs. sucrose alone; (4) a 12-week trial with 7g twice daily showed significant reductions in body fat, BMI, and visceral fat vs. sucralose control at the same caloric intake. The proposed mechanisms are three-pronged: (a) allulose occupies GLUT2 transporters in the gut wall, limiting glucose absorption; (b) it translocates glucokinase from liver cell nuclei to the cytoplasm, activating glucose uptake and glycogen storage; (c) it promotes GLP-1 release. A single-dose study also found a measurable shift toward fat oxidation and away from carb oxidation after one serving.

Andrew Huberman — Dr. Andy Galpin (Strength & Hypertrophy Essentials)

Galpin's key practical claims: strength loss with aging (~2–4%/year) outpaces muscle loss (~1%/year), and power loss (~8–10%/year) is the real functional threat — the ability to catch a fall, not just bicep size. Andrew Huberman — Dr. Arthur Brooks (Happiness)

Brooks argues happiness is not a feeling but a composite of three macronutrients: enjoyment (pleasure elevated by people and memory, managed rather than consuming), satisfaction (the specific joy that comes after struggle — not comfort), and meaning (your why). The critical and underappreciated point: unhappiness lives inside all three macronutrients — discontent and frustration are features, not bugs, and people who spend their lives avoiding suffering paradoxically miss happiness. The distinction between pleasure (limbic, animal, dopamine-driven, manageable into addiction) and enjoyment (pleasure plus social connection plus memory plus voluntary executive control) is his sharpest conceptual line. High-achievers specifically tend to confuse wanting to be special with wanting to be happy, which causes them to deprioritize the enjoyment macronutrient entirely.

🧬 Health & Science

GLP-1 Body Composition Problem: The Cycling Risk

From an Huberman short with a physician guest: cycling on and off GLP-1 drugs (like semaglutide) creates a ratcheting body composition problem. Roughly 1/3 of weight lost on GLP-1s is lean mass (muscle). When patients stop and regain weight, they regain fat — not lean mass. Each on/off cycle therefore shifts body composition toward a higher fat-to-muscle ratio. The proposed solution is either microdosing (finding the lowest dose that maintains weight without continued lean mass loss) or having a structured bridging plan before discontinuation. This is a clinically significant point for anyone considering GLP-1s.

ADHD as a Circadian Rhythm Disorder

A 2025 paper in Frontiers in Psychiatry (307 HN points) argues ADHD should be understood as primarily a circadian rhythm disorder, not just a dopamine/attention disorder. The implication is that "chronotherapy" — timing interventions (light, sleep schedule, medication) to align with circadian biology — could be a meaningful treatment lever that's currently underutilized. This reframing has practical consequences for when medications are administered and how sleep hygiene is prioritized in ADHD treatment.

Peter Attia — Biology of Pregnancy as a Stress Test

In a conversation with OB/GYN researcher Dr. Katie Harbath, Attia frames pregnancy as one of the most extreme physiologic stress tests a body undergoes: plasma volume expands 50% by 28 weeks (causing relative anemia, not compensatory hemoglobin increase); the placenta takes over sex hormone production from the ovaries at ~8–10 weeks; insulin resistance rises substantially in the second half of pregnancy and can unmask underlying metabolic predisposition. Women with severe enough cardiac or pulmonary disease literally cannot tolerate the 50% plasma expansion — it would cause heart failure. The episode covers GDF-15 as the likely driver of hyperemesis gravidarum (rather than hCG as previously assumed), which is now a therapeutic target in trials.

Stoicism and Wealth

Massimo Pigliucci (Figs in Winter) examines the genuine tension in Stoic thought between the doctrine that externals like wealth are "preferred indifferents" (not truly good or bad, but reasonably preferred) and the way luxury and wealth practically corrupt character. The Stoics were not ascetics, but they were deeply suspicious of attachment to externals — a distinction worth sitting with if you're building wealth while trying to maintain equanimity.

Stoic Cost-Benefit Analysis

Donald Robertson explains how the Stoics evaluated actions not just by outcome but by cost to character — what it does to your inner state, not just your external situation. The practical framing: before an action, ask not only "what do I gain?" but "what does this cost my character if I do it, or if I don't?" A useful mental model for decisions under pressure.
