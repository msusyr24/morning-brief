Worth your time:

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz breaks down a new Nature Aging study that took the most detailed molecular snapshot of aging muscle ever — 24,000+ gene readouts, 135 metabolites, 1,383 lipids across young, trained older, normally active older, and physically impaired older adults. The headline finding: highly trained older adults had 57% of age-related gene expression changes simply absent — not slowed, gone. The preserved gains were concentrated specifically in mitochondrial energy machinery (NAD metabolism, electron transport chain complexes 1 and 4), suggesting exercise isn't just building a reserve you spend later, it's keeping the cellular engine running on a younger blueprint. Qualification: "trained" in this study meant ≥3 sessions/week, ≥1 hour each — about 3 hours/week total, a realistic bar. He also flags that chronically elevated blood sugar makes muscles less able to remodel in response to exercise (via glycation, inflammation, impaired angiogenesis) — a mechanism explaining why some people are "exercise non-responders."

Jared Alderman

Short but sharp PLO theory clip: the core insight is that when holding the current nuts (e.g., 8-9 on a J-T-7 board without a flush draw), the right question isn't "do I have the best hand now?" but "what's the probability this hand is still the nuts by the river?" Without backup equity, the solver actually checks back nut straights at a frequency — because betting to protect a hand that has a high chance of being counterfeited is a fear-based pattern, not an EV-maximizing one. Top set bets more than a naked straight because top set retains equity even when called by sets that improve. Classic case of solver output running counter to instinct.

🧬 Health & Science

Pulse Pressure: The Blood Pressure Number Doctors Aren't Measuring

Multiple sources this cycle converged on an under-discussed cardiovascular marker. Dr. Sten Ekberg made the clearest case: pulse pressure (systolic − diastolic) reflects arterial elasticity, and a PP of 30-40 is ideal — a 115/55 reading (PP=60) is worse than 128/88 (PP=40) despite looking better by conventional metrics. The mechanism: stiff arteries (from elastin degradation via glycation, inflammation, mechanical fatigue over ~3 billion heartbeats) force the heart to pump harder, creating left ventricular hypertrophy, reducing coronary artery perfusion (which happens during diastole, fed by aortic rebound), and sending abrupt pressure waves to kidneys and brain. Notably, the 2025 blood pressure guidelines didn't mention pulse pressure at all, focusing entirely on absolute numbers and earlier drug intervention. Peter Attia's rebroadcast reinforced the broader point: blood pressure is one of only three primary causes of atherosclerosis (alongside apoB and smoking), and most people either don't know their numbers or measure them incorrectly (office readings are unreliable; the SPRINT trial used a 5-minute seated rest protocol with three consecutive readings).

Exercise Preserves Mitochondrial Youth at the Gene Expression Level

Nick Norwitz's breakdown of the Nature Aging paper is the most compelling health finding in this cycle. The study found 57% of age-related gene expression changes in muscle were absent in highly trained older adults — specifically in mitochondrial energy metabolism genes (NAD biosynthesis, electron transport chain complexes). This reframes the exercise-longevity story: it's not primarily about building a muscle reserve that you draw down, it's about maintaining the quality and function of cellular energy systems. Andrew Huberman's mitochondria episode with Dr. Jared Rutter provides the mechanistic backdrop: mitochondria generate reactive oxygen species when overpowered with excess energy (from overfeeding or metabolic dysfunction), leading to genomic damage and protein damage — one of the core drivers of the aging process at the cellular level. The convergence: the same mitochondria that degrade with sedentary aging are specifically preserved by exercise.

Fasting Insulin as a Leading Indicator — A Clinician's Perspective

Ken Berry's clinical observation is worth flagging as a standalone health data point: a fasting insulin above 15 can predict type 2 diabetes 5-15 years before it manifests, well before fasting glucose or HbA1c enter pre-diabetic ranges. Dr. Sten Ekberg's blood pressure video reinforced this with a specific example: a patient with BP of 135/85 and an A1C of 5.6 (below pre-diabetic threshold) could have a fasting insulin of 12-14 and a HOMA-IR (homeostatic model of insulin resistance) of ~2.0, indicating significant insulin resistance and likely subclinical atherosclerosis — yet standard guidelines would only flag the blood pressure and prescribe medication. The argument across both sources: treating blood pressure pharmacologically without addressing insulin resistance as the underlying driver is a band-aid that misses the actual causal mechanism.
