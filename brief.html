Worth your time:

GTO Lab (Nick Petrangelo, Daniel Dvoress & Axel Hallay)

This is a genuinely useful deep-dive into mystery bounty tournament strategy — the core argument is that most players treat bounty stages as "play a little looser" when in reality it's a completely different game with different mathematics. The key concept introduced is the KO-to-stack ratio (bounty value ÷ current stack), which drives all pre- and post-flop strategy adjustments. They walk through a GTO Lab library of ~300 sims covering various stack depths and bounty sizes, and show that at the earliest bounty stage with large fields, players should sometimes VPIP 100% from early positions — not because they're gambling, but because the KO equity (shown in yellow in the EV breakdown tool) makes otherwise-negative plays clearly profitable. The most common leak they identify: being too anchored to vanilla tournament thinking and not hunting bounties aggressively enough.

Video: Mystery Bounty Is a Completely Different Game | Nick Petrangelo, Daniel Dvoress & Axel Hallay

Jared Alderman (feat. Uri Peleg)

Uri Peleg makes a sharp, underappreciated point: GTO poker assumes both players know each other's game tree, but in practice your opponent has no idea what your actual strategy is — which means you can "fudge" your frequencies in ways that are unexploitable as long as you're globally in the ballpark. His concrete example: you can over-bluff ace-high boards and under-bluff king-high boards, and no opponent can detect this without you telling them. The broader frame is the chess analogy — your range is an invisible chessboard, and exploitative poker is making an informed guess about how your opponent has set up theirs. Jared also floats an interesting theoretical idea: randomizing bet *sizes* (not just frequencies) as a hidden information advantage, which Uri finds impractical but conceptually interesting for creating asymmetric game-tree knowledge.

Video: "Poker Doesn't Work Without Exploits" - Uri Peleg

Lex Fridman (feat. mathematicians on AI & math)

This episode is a serious conversation about what it means that AI (specifically OpenAI's recent release) has apparently solved or made major progress on Navier-Stokes, a quasi-Riemann Hypothesis result, the unique games conjecture, and hundreds of other problems. The mathematicians are careful to distinguish between "convex hull" progress (recombining existing ideas in ways humans could have done) versus genuinely new mathematical ideas — their consensus is the AI is doing something closer to the "semigroup" model: tiling known moves far further than humans have explored, not necessarily inventing new definitions. Terence Tao's concern is cited directly: PhD students lose motivation when they know a problem is solved, and the entire ecosystem of patient, slow research is disrupted. The "doorman fallacy" framing is worth keeping — reducing a mathematician's job to "proving theorems" misses everything else they do (asking the right questions, building new conceptual structures, mentorship). Practically, the panelists think the role of humans shifts toward question-asking, cross-domain connection-making, and guiding what matters — but acknowledge this is harder to operationalize for grad students right now.

🧬 Health & Science

Fat Cells Store Glycogen — and It Drives Insulin Resistance (DeLauer / Molecular Metabolism)

A Molecular Metabolism study found that metabolically dysfunctional people store substantially more glycogen in adipose tissue than lean individuals. When fat tissue grows faster than its blood supply (as in obesity), low-oxygen conditions cause fat cells to take up more glucose and store it as glycogen rather than converting it to triglycerides — and those glycogen-laden fat cells release inflammatory signals linked to insulin resistance. An American Journal of Physiology study found that endurance-trained men's fat tissue responds better to insulin (not just their muscles), suggesting adipose insulin sensitivity is trainable. Practical protocol: resistance + interval training, delay carb refeeding 2-3 hours post-workout, concentrate carbs in one or two meals rather than grazing — all aimed at keeping the transitional glycogen pool in fat cells low and breaking the inflammatory feedback loop.

Huberman: Exercise → Alertness Window via Parallel Pathways

Moderate-to-high intensity exercise releases adrenal epinephrine, which binds vagus nerve receptors and triggers norepinephrine release from the locus coeruleus — creating elevated alertness for hours post-exercise (provided you don't train to exhaustion). The key practical point: exercise doesn't make you smarter on its own; it creates an alertness window you must deliberately fill with focused learning to drive neuroplasticity. Cold exposure triggers a parallel pathway — peripheral dopamine/epinephrine → locus coeruleus norepinephrine — producing similar multi-hour alertness plus a central dopamine rise in mesolimbic reward circuits.

When Claude Code's "don't ask again" permission feature is used, it writes sensitive credentials and API keys directly into config files that developers then accidentally commit to public GitHub repos. Robertson explains the REBT-derived technique of mentally rehearsing disturbing scenarios while actively changing your emotional response — not just your cognitions.
