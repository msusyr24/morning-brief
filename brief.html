Mental Model · Epistemics

Survivorship Bias

We see the winners and forget the losers who didn't make it. Successful entrepreneurs' advice is biased toward the strategies of survivors, ignoring the identical strategies of people who failed. WWII example: engineers wanted to armor the parts of returning planes with bullet holes — until Abraham Wald pointed out the planes that didn't return were hit elsewhere.

Jared Alderman

Uri Peleg makes a sharp point that reframes GTO balance in practice: the question isn't whether you're perfectly balanced, it's whether your opponent can detect that you're not. You can systematically over-bluff certain board textures and under-bluff others — as long as you're in the global ballpark of correct frequencies — and no opponent can practically discover the deviation unless you tell them. This reframes "hidden information" as a strategic asset: knowing where you are in your game tree when your opponent doesn't is a genuine edge. The conversation also touches on randomizing bet sizing as a way to make checkbacks more ambiguous, though Uri notes mastering a single-size game tree is hard enough for most players that multi-sizing remains more conceptual than practical at most levels.

GTO Lab

Nick Petrangelo, Daniel Dvoress, and Axel Hallay walk through a new Mystery Bounty library with purpose-built sims — a format where almost nobody plays correctly because the game tree is so large and the bounty math so non-obvious. Key insight: you need to split EV into two components (KO pool EV vs. prize pool EV) and see how they blend — some hands that are clearly losing tournament chips are still profitable calls when you layer in the knockout value. Axel introduces the "KO stack ratio" (bounty value ÷ opponent's stack) as a practical shortcut: if the ratio exceeds 2, call any two cards. The library covers stages from 1,000 players left to the final table, showing how the push-pull between hunting bounties and playing for the prize pool shifts dramatically across stages — which is exactly why everyone's playing these wrong.

Thomas DeLauer

Two substantive metabolism videos this period. First: the post-meal timeline broken down hour by hour — the cephalic phase (your brain primes insulin, bile, and enzymes before the first bite via the vagus nerve, and distracted eating blunts this), the incretin effect (60% of your insulin response is triggered by gut hormones GLP-1 and GIP before blood sugar even rises), and a key finding from the American Journal of Physiology showing a 190-calorie snack eaten at 11pm significantly reduced overnight fat oxidation compared to the same snack at 10am — same food, same person, only the clock changed. His practical takeaways: eat fat/protein before carbs to prime incretins, walk 10-30 min post-meal for glucose (muscle contraction pulls glucose in without insulin), and finish eating 2-3 hours before bed to protect the overnight fat-burning window. Second video argues that habitual fasting at the same time each day trains ghrelin to spike anticipatorily via orexin neurons — making hunger a learned clock, not a fuel gauge. The fix: rotate your break-fast time within a 2-3 hour window to prevent the conditioned spike from consolidating, and ride out the 15-20 minute fade window when it does hit.

Peter Attia (The Drive)

Conversation with oncologist Siddhartha Mukherjee (author of The Emperor of All Maladies) surveying 15 years of cancer progress. Most striking update: the Vogelstein framing of cancer mutations as ~5% hereditary / ~30% environmental / ~65% random ("bad luck") is being revised upward on the hereditary side — not because new single-gene mutations were found, but because polygenic "nudge mutations" (hundreds of small-effect genes stacking risk) account for a large fraction of hereditary cancers that BRCA1/2 alone couldn't explain. On treatment: immunotherapy now meaningfully extends survival in some lung cancer patients (something Mukherjee says he would not have believed possible 15 years ago), CAR-T has produced 15-year survivors in some leukemias/lymphomas, and the KRAS-targeting drug Adagrasib is generating real (not just weeks-long) survival gains in pancreatic cancer for the first time. On early detection: liquid biopsies (blood-based cancer tests) likely don't work well on unselected general populations due to false positive rates, but may be valuable in high-genetic-risk cohorts.

ModernWisdom / Dr. K (HealthyGamer)

Dr. K (Alok Kanojia) recounts his path from failing out of college on 16-20 hours of gaming per day, to 90 days in an Indian ashram, to being told by his spiritual teacher to go back and "become exceptional in the material world first." Bitcoin Core Ends Long Embargoes for Bugs AI Can Rediscover

Bitcoin Core is changing its vulnerability disclosure policy: bugs that AI systems could plausibly rediscover independently will no longer be held under long embargo. The reasoning is that a long embargo on a vulnerability an AI could find provides false security — once an AI discovers it, the assumption of secrecy collapses. This is a quiet but meaningful intersection of AI capability and Bitcoin's security model.

🧬 Health & Science

Alzheimer's: 2 Hours of Sleep Restored Without Clearing Brain Plaques

Researchers reported restoring approximately 2 hours of lost sleep in Alzheimer's model subjects without clearing amyloid plaques — the finding is significant because it challenges the assumed causal direction (plaques → sleep loss) and suggests sleep disruption in Alzheimer's may have a parallel, plaque-independent mechanism that could be targeted therapeutically. If replicated, this separates "fixing sleep" from "clearing plaques" as distinct intervention tracks.

Peter Attia × Mukherjee: Specific Cancer Prevention & Detection Takeaways

Beyond the broad landscape summary: Mukherjee flags colorectal cancer rising in young people as a genuine mystery (alcohol and tobacco don't explain it), esophageal cancer rising (probably alcohol × tobacco synergy), and endometrial cancer rising likely due to obesity rates. On liquid biopsies: the problem isn't sensitivity, it's specificity in low-prevalence populations — false positives cascade into unnecessary procedures, so the near-term use case is high-risk (genetic mutation carriers, prior cancer history) rather than broad screening. On metabolic therapies: he flags early-stage science on exploiting cancer's metabolic differences from normal cells as a legitimate and underexplored treatment angle.

Gaius Blossius, the Revolutionary Stoic

Massimo Pigliucci pushes back on the popular caricature of Stoicism as quietist or politically passive — Gaius Blossius was a Stoic philosopher who actively supported the Gracchan reforms and fled Rome rather than submit to what he saw as tyranny. The piece argues that Stoic ethics doesn't demand withdrawal from civic life but rather demands principled engagement with it. Useful corrective to the modern "just control your reactions" reduction of the philosophy.

Stoic Premeditation of Anger

Donald Robertson covers the Stoic practice of premeditatio applied specifically to anger — anticipating provocations in advance as a prophylactic rather than trying to suppress anger once it's already triggered. The core claim, rooted in Seneca, is that anger is far easier to prevent than to stop mid-stream, and that mentally rehearsing likely irritants reduces their impact when they arrive. Practical and well-sourced from the primary texts.
