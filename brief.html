Mental Model · Economics/Productivity

Pareto Principle (80/20)

Roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes, across almost every domain. 80% of sales from 20% of customers, 80% of crime from 20% of criminals, 80% of your happiness from 20% of your activities. The skill: identifying the 20% before wasting effort on the 80%.

Jared Alderman (feat. Uri Peleg)

A genuinely interesting conversation on hidden information as a strategic asset in poker. Uri's core point: GTO balance only matters insofar as your opponent can detect and exploit imbalances — if they can't tell you're over-bluffing ace-high boards and under-bluffing king-high boards, you're globally fine. He frames ranges as "invisible chessboards" and argues that knowing where you are in your game tree, when your opponent doesn't, is an underappreciated edge. The discussion also lands on a practical ceiling: even top nosebleed players don't have multi-street, multi-size game trees mapped out, so the exploits from that hidden asymmetry are larger than most assume. Uri also pushes back on RNG-ing for balance — his point being that if your opponent can't detect your frequencies, you don't need to randomize.

GTO Lab

Nikki P and Axel walk through GTO Lab's new Mystery Bounty library (~240+ sims) with tools purpose-built for the format. The key strategic insight: when bounties first kick in, the dominant mistake is playing too tight — some spots call for 100% VPIP from early position. The point Nikki keeps returning to: Mystery Bounty isn't "a regular tournament with slight adjustments" — it's a structurally different game, and most players are leaving money on the table by treating it like the former.

Lex Fridman

An emergency panel of mathematicians (Brunton, Moitra, Alex) reacting to OpenAI dropping claimed proofs of Navier-Stokes, a quasi-Riemann Hypothesis result, the Unique Games Conjecture, and hundreds more. The consensus: the results appear to be operating in the "semigroup" of known ideas — not the derogatory "convex hull" (trivial combinations), but a vast combinatorial reach of existing techniques assembled in ways no human had time to. Terence Tao's lament about PhD students gets real airtime: once a proof exists, the exploratory journey is gone, and the grad school blueprint ("improve a problem people care about, get papers") is broken. The most useful frame introduced is the "doorman fallacy" — we've confused "proving theorems" (the visible task) with everything a mathematician actually does: asking the right questions, building generalizable structures, exposing beauty. The panel also flags that the Anthropic-connected 3SUM result may be more field-altering than the splashier OpenAI drops because it upends the entire program of fine-grained complexity theory.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia: Who Is Actually at Risk for CTE?

Attia examines what autopsy-based CTE studies can and cannot tell us — a methodologically important distinction since CTE can only be diagnosed post-mortem, meaning all prevalence data comes from brains donated by symptomatic athletes or their families (severe selection bias). The piece is a useful corrective to both overclaiming (every football player will get CTE) and dismissal (it's rare), parsing what repetitive head impact exposure levels actually correlate with pathology vs. just neuropathological markers without clinical significance.

peterattiamd.com

Thomas DeLauer: Creatine Dosing Revision

DeLauer's personal n=1 data point worth noting: moving from microdose creatine (~3-5g) to 15g/day produced noticeably different strength and all-day energy outcomes. He cites literature supporting safe dosing up to 30-40g, though he doesn't personally go that high. The key practical flag: initial water retention subsided after ~one month — the common reason people abandon higher doses prematurely. He also notes that EAAs during training may blunt the body's own muscle-preservation signaling, making it easier to lose muscle when you come off them.

Brazilian Court Sanctions Lawyers for Prompt Injection in Court Filing

Lawyers in Brazil were formally sanctioned after submitting a court filing that contained what appears to be a prompt injection attack — instructions embedded in the document intended to manipulate AI systems reading it. This is the first known case of a court imposing consequences for prompt injection in a legal context, and it sets a precedent for AI-assisted legal workflows becoming an attack surface.

Are LLMs good or bad Bayesians?

Bryan Caplan raises a sharp point: a proper Bayesian, when presented with a counterargument, should update toward or away from the presenter's view based purely on argument quality — not automatically toward the speaker. LLMs appear to systematically move toward whoever is arguing with them, which is a form of sycophancy dressed as open-mindedness. Relevant for anyone using LLMs as thinking partners or sounding boards.
