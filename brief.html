Mental Model · Risk/Mathematics

Convexity vs. Concavity

Convex payoffs benefit from volatility (options, bets with capped losses). Concave payoffs are hurt by volatility (leverage, short-term trading). Life structures that are convex compound in your favor; concave structures blow up eventually. Structure your life to be convex where possible.

Jared Alderman

Alderman interviews high-stakes heads-up player Uri Peleg on a genuinely underexplored idea: how much does it matter if your opponent doesn't know your game tree? Uri's key insight — borrowed from his own experience not RNG'ing — is that balance only matters if your opponent can detect the imbalance. If you over-bluff ace-high boards and under-bluff king-high boards but are globally within range, there's no practical way for opponents to discover it. They discuss whether randomizing bet sizes (rather than just frequencies) could further obscure information, with Uri noting that while theoretically interesting, even top players struggle to master a single-size game tree. The conversation pivots to a chess analogy of "invisible boards" — your range as a chessboard your opponent can't see — which reframes exploitative poker as making informed guesses about how your opponent has set up his pieces.

Lex Fridman

An "emergency episode" assembling three mathematicians to process OpenAI's recent drop of ~700 claimed proofs, including results on Navier-Stokes, a quasi-Riemann Hypothesis, the Exponential Time Hypothesis, and the Unique Games Conjecture. The mathematicians largely agree the proofs represent a "semigroup" of known moves — not a derogatory assessment, since that semigroup can reach much further than any human has explored — rather than genuine new mathematical ideas ("moving the balloon"). Nick Norwitz

A detailed mechanistic breakdown of nitric oxide (NO) and cardiovascular health, with seven actionable strategies. Key specific findings: (1) Regular mouthwash use kills oral bacteria that convert dietary nitrate → nitrite → NO, raising blood pressure by 2–3.5 mmHg in studies — Norwitz stopped using mouthwash for this reason. (2) Humming increases nasal NO production ~15-fold vs. quiet exhalation, via mechanical oscillation of air in the paranasal sinuses. (3) Beetroot juice at 520–1,040mg dietary nitrate produced 12–14% improvements on endurance exercise tasks in a controlled trial. (4) Nasal vs. mouth breathing lowers diastolic blood pressure ~4 mmHg. Regular exercise upregulates NO synthase enzyme expression as a long-term adaptation, reducing blood pressure by 3.5–8+ mmHg. He also attempted to AI-design a low-carb nitric-oxide-boosting brownie using beetroot.

Thomas DeLauer

DeLauer's core argument: high-protein dieters eating conventional muscle meat have a structural ~10g/day glycine deficit, because glycine synthesis (~3g/day endogenous + ~1–2g dietary) can't meet a ~15g/day metabolic demand — and muscle meat provides almost none. A Journal of Biosciences paper mapped all glycine biosynthetic and consumption pathways and concluded glycine should be reclassified as "conditionally essential." The practical consequence: glycine is simultaneously required for creatine synthesis, collagen turnover, and glutathione production — so when scarce, one system loses (usually connective tissue repair or antioxidant capacity). His fix: 15g collagen or bone broth daily to close the gap, supplemental creatine to spare glycine from being used for endogenous creatine synthesis, and timing collagen + vitamin C pre-workout or pre-bed (glycine acts on the circadian clock to accelerate sleep onset).

Renaissance Periodization (Dr. Mike)

Dr. Mike breaks down what makes Alan Ritchson's physique effective for an action-hero look: outsized chest and delts that create a "Dorito chip" silhouette, making even large arms look proportional. His programming prescription for replicating this: chest gets heavier, lower-rep, lower-frequency training (6–12 reps, high stretch under load); side delts require the opposite — high reps, high volume, near-daily frequency because they don't stretch deeply and the glenohumeral joint can't sustain ultra-heavy frontal-plane loading. Two specific exercise prescriptions: (1) Low incline dumbbell "out press" — at the bottom of a deep stretch, push dumbbells outward (not up) for ~3 seconds before pressing, maximally increasing pec tension at the point of deepest stretch; (2) Standing lateral partial-rep superset — pinkies up, lean 10°, go to failure at parallel, then continue with partial reps until you can't reach 45°. The 5-day program he outlines targets 12–30 weekly chest sets and 14–35 weekly side delt sets at peak volume.

Andrew Huberman & Ross Edgley

A long-form conversation with ultra-endurance athlete Ross Edgley (swam around Great Britain, ~2,000 miles in 157 days) focused on the architecture of motivation. Edgley distinguishes intrinsic-but-ego-driven motivation (his Great Britain swim was about records and prestige — Maslow's esteem needs) from purpose-driven motivation (his Iceland swim collected eDNA for ocean conservation research). The Sisyphus frame he returns to: Camus's reading that "the struggle itself is enough to fill a man's heart" was his mental anchor during the worst passages of the swim.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia: The Neuroscience of Emotion — Lisa Feldman Barrett

Barrett's "predictive processing" theory of emotion directly challenges the conventional view that emotions are universal, hardwired responses triggered by stimuli. Her core claim: emotions are constructions — your brain is constantly making predictions about what incoming sensory signals mean based on past experience, and those predictions are the emotion. The actionable implication is that emotions are not things that happen to you; they are built by you, which means they are modifiable through the concepts and vocabulary you use to categorize experience (more precise emotional granularity = more regulatory control). This reframes emotional regulation as a skill of prediction-updating rather than impulse suppression.

Episode #413 · Peter Attia

Circadian Subtype of Depression: New Research

Researchers have identified a subtype of depression affecting roughly 1 in 4 depressed patients characterized by disruptions to circadian rhythm — distinct enough in its biological signature and symptom profile that it may require different treatment approaches than standard depression protocols. The implication is that a meaningful proportion of treatment-resistant or atypically-presenting depression cases may have a chronobiological root that current standard-of-care largely ignores.

Gaius Blossius, the Revolutionary Stoic — Massimo Pigliucci

Pigliucci profiles Gaius Blossius, Stoic philosopher and advisor to the reformer Tiberius Gracchus, who was executed for his political activism — a direct challenge to the popular caricature of Stoicism as passive acceptance or quietism. The argument: Stoic commitment to justice and virtue has historically demanded engagement with injustice, not withdrawal from it. Useful corrective for anyone who reads Marcus Aurelius primarily as a text about inner peace.

Stoic Premeditation of Anger — Donald Robertson

Robertson examines the Stoic practice of praemeditatio applied specifically to anger and the desire for revenge — the idea that you can anticipate provocations in advance and mentally rehearse your response before the trigger occurs. This is the preventative therapy version of emotional regulation: you don't manage anger reactively but pre-emptively weaken its grip by expecting it. Practical and grounded in the classical sources.
