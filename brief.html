Nick Norwitz

Norwitz covers three distinct ideas worth sitting with. First, a 7-day water-only fast study (12 healthy subjects, ~3,000 proteins tracked) found that 1,034 proteins changed significantly — but the bulk of the change didn't begin until day 3, which he argues explains why most fasting research misses the real biology: studies end at 24-48 hours, before the "software update" kicks in. Proteins linked to cardiovascular risk (HYOU1) dropped significantly; proteins tied to brain scaffolding (tenascin-R, involved in perineuronal nets and memory consolidation) also shifted. Fat loss persisted after refeeding while lean mass largely returned within 3 days. Second, he rates heart health supplements: nattokinase gets S-tier (blood pressure reduction in RCTs, possible plaque reversal), omega-3s A-tier, microplastic-binding probiotics A-tier (citing a NEJM study linking microplastics in arterial plaques to ~4.5x higher cardiovascular event risk). Third, a bowhead whale biology short: the whale's CIRBP protein enables extraordinary DNA repair, dramatically reduces cancer risk despite 1,000x more cells than humans, and when inserted into flies, extended their lifespan — humans make the same protein, raising the question of whether we can safely upregulate it.

Stronger by Science

A new meta-analytic look at the correlation between hypertrophy and strength gains finds the relationship is stronger than prior studies suggested — meaning muscle size contributes more to strength gains in newer lifters than previously thought — but the authors caution that the correlation is nowhere near as tight as a naive reading of the data implies. The practical takeaway: training for size and training for strength are more aligned than the "they're separate" camp argues, especially early in a lifting career, but they're still not the same thing and shouldn't be conflated.

🧬 Health & Science

The Day 3 Fasting Threshold — What Changes After 48 Hours

Nick Norwitz's breakdown of a 7-day water-only fast study (12 subjects, 3,000 proteins tracked) is the most substantive health science of this brief. The key finding: most of the 1,034 proteins that changed significantly over the fast didn't begin shifting until day 3 — meaning the bulk of published fasting research, which cuts off at 24-48 hours, is measuring the fuel-switching phase (glycogen depletion, ketosis onset) and missing the broader "systems remodel." After day 3, proteins tied to cardiovascular risk (HYOU1, associated with ~2x lower CV disease risk per standard deviation decrease), brain structural integrity (tenascin-R, involved in perineuronal nets that stabilize synaptic connections), and immune/inflammatory pathways all shifted. Fat loss (1.85kg) persisted after a 3-day refeed; apparent lean mass loss (~3.6kg during the fast) largely reversed, consistent with glycogen/water depletion rather than muscle protein loss. The "hack" framing in his title refers to his argument that certain fasting mimetics or multi-day protocols may be able to trigger the day-3 proteome shift more efficiently.

Fluffy LDL Is Not Safe — Particle Number Is What Matters

Peter Attia's Jim Otvos clip makes a direct empirical case: patients with familial hypercholesterolemia (who die of heart attacks at 30-35 when homozygous) have large LDL particles, not small ones. The claim that "large fluffy buoyant LDL" is benign even at high particle counts is "fallacious" by Otvos's direct characterization, and the FH data is the clearest counterexample. The actionable takeaway: if your LDL-P (particle number) is above ~2,000 nmol/L, particle size does not rescue you.

Alcohol: No Net Health Benefit at Any Intake Level

A June 2026 U.S. government-commissioned meta-analysis in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs found no clear evidence that alcohol provides a net health benefit at any dose. While low-to-moderate drinking showed marginally favorable associations with ischemic heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and dementia in some analyses, these were outweighed by increased cancer risk (breast, colorectal, esophageal, pancreatic), liver disease, and all-cause mortality. Seven drinks/week was already associated with a detectable net harm; 14/week (~1 drink/day x 14 days) was associated with roughly 1 alcohol-attributable death per 25 people over a lifetime. Mechanistically: ethanol metabolizes to acetaldehyde (DNA-damaging), disrupts sleep architecture, raises blood pressure, and the "heart-protective" HDL effects don't sum to net positive when cancer and injury risk are included. Dr. Mike explicitly updated his prior position from "1-2 drinks/day is probably neutral" to "alcohol is a net negative at all doses."

Creatine Is Primarily Anti-Catabolic, Not Anabolic — and Most Needed During Cuts

Thomas DeLauer summarizes the mechanism: creatine increases intracellular osmotic load, drawing water into muscle cells (cell swelling), which signals the body to suppress protein breakdown (proteolysis) rather than stimulating new synthesis. A 2001 study in Diabetes showed creatine increased GLUT4 content by ~40% during immobilization/rehab — meaning it also preserves glucose uptake capacity in muscle during periods of reduced activity or low glycogen. The counterintuitive implication: creatine is most important when cutting or fasting (when glycogen depletion shrinks cells and triggers catabolism), not when bulking. Pairing 5-10g creatine with a small carbohydrate load (10g) post-workout strengthens the sodium-dependent creatine transporter and maximizes uptake. Adding 0.5-2g taurine (a separate osmolyte, different transporter) further supports cell hydration through a complementary pathway.
