Peter Attia

In a retrospective AMA (episode 411 / 400-episode marker), Attia lays out how his views have shifted on keto, fasting, and nutrition broadly. His biggest reversal: he no longer believes ketogenic diets work via insulin suppression — he now thinks they work for the same reason most restrictive diets work, which is that they make it easier to control total energy intake. 🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia's Evolving Views on Keto and Fasting — Specific Reversals

The most actionable update from Attia's AMA: he has walked back his core mechanistic argument for keto. He previously believed insulin suppression was the active ingredient; he now believes the diet works by improving satiety and reducing total energy intake — full stop. The implication is that keto has no special metabolic magic over other approaches that achieve the same protein intake and energy deficit. On fasting: his quarterly 7–10 day fasts are gone. His current view is that a 12–14 hour overnight fast, combined with exercise, likely captures most of the autophagy benefit at far lower cost. He has not abandoned fasting — he's abandoned the idea that extreme durations add proportional benefit.

Fructose Processing: The Liver-First Model Is Wrong

Dr. Sten Ekberg covers a 2018 finding (mouse studies, confirmed mechanistically in humans) that overturns the Lustig model: the small intestine — not the liver — is the primary site of fructose clearance. At low doses (~10–25g per sitting in humans, context-dependent), up to 90% of fructose is cleared by the gut and never reaches the liver. The liver only handles overflow. Critical variables: liquid sugar is absorbed fastest and saturates gut capacity soonest; a can of Coke (~21g fructose) likely exceeds gut capacity in most people even with the revised model. Whole fruit at low-to-moderate doses (blackberries 2.4g/100g, apples 6.9g/100g) stays under the threshold if consumed slowly. Medjool dates (32g fructose/100g) and most sweetened beverages are still clearly problematic. Fructose is still harmful via blunting of ghrelin suppression and lack of insulin response, but the mechanism is more nuanced than "liver gets overwhelmed."

Three Distinct Autophagy Pathways: Stacking Exercise, Fasting, and Urolithin A

Thomas DeLauer synthesizes the mechanism literature: exercise triggers autophagy via AMPK→ULK1 (essential — knocking either out blocks exercise-induced autophagy entirely), with peak mitophagy occurring ~6 hours post-session, not during training. Fasting works via mTOR suppression + BNIP3 pathway — a different molecular route. Urolithin A (postbiotic from pomegranate ellagitannins, produced by gut bacteria) activates PINK1/Parkin, a selective mitochondria-tagging pathway; an RCT in JAMA found significant improvements in muscular endurance in both hand and leg muscles with 1,000mg urolithin A. The practical stack: high-intensity resistance training in a fasted state (morning), remain fasted ~6 hours post-session, 14–16hr overnight fast, urolithin A with evening meal. Only ~12% of people initially have the microbiome to extract urolithin A from pomegranates, but consistent consumption develops the relevant bacteria (Akkermansia) over time.

Stoic Cost-Benefit Analysis — Donald Robertson

Robertson explicates the Stoic practice of weighing actions not by outcome probability but by what the choice costs your character. The frame: every action either builds or erodes the kind of person you're becoming, and the Stoics treated that cost as the primary accounting — external outcomes are secondary. Practically useful as a decision heuristic for situations where the stakes feel low but the pattern of behavior compounds.

Donald Robertson / Substack

Stoicism and Wealth — Massimo Pigliucci

Pigliucci works through the genuine tension in Stoic ethics around externals like wealth: the Stoics classified it as a "preferred indifferent" — not good in itself, but reasonably pursued when it doesn't compromise virtue. The essay distinguishes between wealth as a tool and wealth as an end, and examines where historical Stoics (Seneca especially, who was very rich) navigated this. Useful for anyone thinking seriously about Stoicism in a context where financial success is real.

Pessimism Always Sounds Smarter Than Optimism — Morgan Housel

Housel's recurring thesis: pessimism signals intelligence and sophistication in social contexts because it appears well-reasoned and cautious, while optimism reads as naïve. The structural problem this creates is that negative priors get disproportionate attention and credibility relative to their accuracy. Short but a useful mental model to have loaded when consuming financial or health media.
