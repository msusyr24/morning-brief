Worth your time:

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz highlights a randomized controlled trial in which participants with obesity were assigned to a high-fat ketogenic diet, a Mediterranean diet, or a low-fat plant-based diet, with weight loss controlled to ~10% over 6 months. Despite the keto group consuming 7x more saturated fat and 50x more cholesterol, LDL and apoB dropped by similar amounts across keto and plant-based groups. Beyond cholesterol, the keto group showed superior improvements in blood sugar, liver fat, inflammation, and insulin resistance — directly challenging the framing that saturated fat drives cardiovascular risk independent of metabolic context.

Videos: MORE Saturated Fat and LOWER LDL Cholesterol?

Renaissance Periodization (Dr. Mike)

Dr. Mike does a thorough literature review breakdown of the carnivore diet, separating real benefits (protein satiety, removal of hyperpalatable foods, glycogen loss driving early weight loss, elimination of individual intolerances) from what he argues are the actual drivers — not "carnivore" per se, but higher protein, fewer ultra-processed foods, and food monotony. The concerning side: dramatic LDL-C and ApoB increases in many adherents, gout flares, kidney stone risk, no fiber, performance declines (especially sprint and volume work), and poor long-term sustainability. His core argument: nearly all claimed carnivore benefits can be achieved through other dietary strategies without the lipid and flexibility downsides. On alcohol, he provides a practical dose-response framing: 1 drink twice a week ≈ almost no impact on gains; 2 drinks 5x/week = noticeable but modest impact; 6+ drinks daily = significantly worse results.

Videos: The Carnivore Diet Is Worse Than We Thought (ugly new data) · How Much Alcohol Actually Hurts Your Gains

Thomas DeLauer

DeLauer makes a specific, mechanism-based case against standard multivitamin formulations. Four key conflicts: (1) Up to 60% of people carry MTHFR variants reducing folic acid conversion by 35–70% — fix is to look for methylfolate/5-MTHF on the label instead; (2) Alpha-tocopherol (standard vitamin E) displaces gamma-tocopherol by ~58% in trials, removing the form that actually inhibits inflammatory COX-2 pathways; (3) Iron and zinc share intestinal transporters — at a 25:1 ratio, zinc absorption drops 42%, though this disappears when taken with food; (4) Vitamin A and D compete for RXR receptors at the gene-expression level, not absorption — excess vitamin A blunts vitamin D's calcium and immune effects. His cortisol/visceral fat video cites a Biological Psychiatry study showing a single daily stressor reduces resting energy expenditure by 104 calories and shifts calories toward visceral fat storage — adding up to ~11 lbs/year. Interventions with data: L-theanine (200mg) cut salivary cortisol 42.4% in a randomized triple-blind trial; dark chocolate (500mg flavonoids) inhibits the enzyme converting cortisone to active cortisol in tissues; saffron (14–28mg) raises melatonin via serotonin conversion and has GABA-modulating properties.

Videos: This Study Just Threw a Wrench in Multivitamins · This Drops Visceral Fat WITHOUT Changing Your Diet (11lbs)

🧬 Health & Science

Multivitamin Formulation Conflicts — Specific Mechanisms

Thomas DeLauer's video this week is unusually mechanism-dense and worth a dedicated flag. The folic acid/MTHFR issue affects an estimated 60% of the population and results in unmetabolized folic acid accumulating in blood — confirmed in a BMC Public Health study where 400mcg/day supplementation already exceeded conversion capacity before adding fortified food. The vitamin E displacement finding is counterintuitive: standard alpha-tocopherol supplements (even natural forms) reduce circulating gamma-tocopherol by a median 58% (n=184 RCT, 2 months), potentially explaining why vitamin E trials keep failing for cardiovascular outcomes. Practical upshots: look for methylfolate on labels; avoid standalone vitamin E supplements; take multivitamins with food to neutralize the iron/zinc transporter competition; be cautious about stacking preformed vitamin A with vitamin D supplementation.

Thomas DeLauer — This Study Just Threw a Wrench in Multivitamins

Ketogenic Diet vs. Plant-Based: Weight-Loss-Controlled RCT on LDL and Metabolic Markers

Nick Norwitz's summary of a recent RCT is the strongest data point of the week for the low-carb vs. conventional debate. With weight loss held constant at ~10% over 6 months, both the keto and low-fat plant-based diets lowered LDL and ApoB by similar amounts — despite the keto group eating 7x more saturated fat and 50x more cholesterol. The keto group additionally showed superior results on blood glucose, liver fat, inflammatory markers, and insulin resistance. This suggests that when you control for weight loss, saturated fat's effect on LDL becomes secondary to the metabolic context, and that cardiovascular surrogate markers improve across both approaches via weight reduction rather than macronutrient composition per se.

Nick Norwitz — MORE Saturated Fat and LOWER LDL Cholesterol?

Cortisol and Visceral Fat: The 104-Calorie/Day Energy Expenditure Finding

The Biological Psychiatry study cited by DeLauer is worth flagging on its own: a single daily psychosocial stressor reduced resting energy expenditure by 104 calories, spiked insulin, and shifted caloric storage toward visceral fat — in people eating identical diets. Compounded over a year, that's ~11 lbs of fat with zero change in food intake. The L-theanine cortisol reduction (42.4% in salivary cortisol post-stressor, randomized triple-blind) is among the more robust acute data points for an over-the-counter supplement. The saffron sleep data (14–28mg before bed, 28-day trial, n=120) showed improvements in sleep onset and quality via serotonin→melatonin pathway with GABA modulation, with 14mg and 28mg performing similarly.

LDL Cumulative Exposure and the Case for Early Intervention (Peter Attia / Michael Davidson)

Davidson's "8 gram rule" is a useful reframe: 200 mg/dL LDL × 40 years = 8g lifetime cholesterol exposure associated with heart disease onset; keeping LDL at 80 mg/dL lifelong may prevent disease entirely. His personal comparison — he started statins in med school after his father's death at 47, his brother went vegetarian instead and had bypass at 44 — is a striking n=1. The broader argument mirrors Attia's long-standing position: LDL is causal (Mendelian randomization + multiple drug-class trials confirm this), and the time to treat a causal risk factor is independent of short-term risk horizon, the same logic applied uncontroversially to blood pressure and smoking cessation.
