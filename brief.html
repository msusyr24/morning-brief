GTO Wizard

A sharp walkthrough of GTO Wizard's new preflop ICM solver, using a real final-table hand as the case study: second-in-chips jams QJs over a chip-leader 3-bet and busts fourth, costing ~$60K vs. a second-place finish. The solver confirms the jam is a significant mistake — the cutoff should be playing a strictly 4-bet-or-fold strategy (no calling range), and even modeling the chip leader as an aggressive "bully" (3-betting 5% more than GTO) doesn't flip QJs to a profitable shove. Key insight: when you know the big stack will 3-bet aggressively, the correct adjustment is often to open less, not to open with the intention of jamming over the 3-bet. The video also demonstrates node-locking to stress-test assumptions, and shows why the chip leader's 7.5x 3-bet size (vs. GTO's preferred ~6x) is suboptimal in equilibrium — you give up more when folding to a shove without gaining extra folds, since the cutoff has no calling range at either size anyway.

GTO Lab

Quick but precise conceptual distinction: equity advantage tells you who should be betting, but it does NOT tell you how big you can bet. Nuts advantage — being more "nutted" (having more of the strongest hands) than your opponent — is what licenses large bets. If you have an equity edge but no nuts advantage, you can bet, but you can't bet big. When equity is roughly 50/50, the nuts advantage alone determines betting structure. Worth internalizing as a clean two-variable framework for reading boards quickly.

Chaffee's Q&A surfaced a specific mechanistic argument worth tracking: the hypothesis (argued since the 1980s per his claim) that autoimmunity is driven not by the body attacking itself but by plant lectins binding to tissues, triggering an immune response that hits host cells in the crossfire. Separately, he flags that B12 lab reference ranges are population-based averages of a "largely vegetarian, sick, malnourished" population and argues optimal B12 sits at 800–1200 pmol/L, well above most lab upper limits.
