Peter Attia

In this AMA reflection episode, Attia walks through how several of his core views have changed. Most significantly: he no longer believes ketogenic diets work primarily through insulin suppression — he now thinks they work the same way most restrictive diets work, by making it easier to control energy intake and increasing satiety. The corollary is that hyperinsulinemia is downstream of energy imbalance, not the primary driver — fixing insulin without fixing energy balance won't resolve metabolic dysfunction. On extended fasting, he did quarterly 7–10 day fasts plus monthly 3-day fasts for years, motivated by autophagy research showing ~3 days of fasting meaningfully elevates autophagy above what exercise alone produces; he's since wound this down, citing the cost-to-benefit tradeoff as the evidence base matured. His current position: protein target (≥1.4g/kg) matters more than the dietary strategy used to hit it.

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz unpacks a study published in Cell showing that stimulant medications (Adderall, Ritalin) don't improve attention networks at all — brain imaging of ~12,000 children found stimulants acted on sensory/motor and reward networks, not the attention circuitry everyone assumed they were repairing. The key implication: for many children, stimulants appear to be compensating for chronic sleep deprivation rather than treating a neurological attention deficit — well-rested kids who took stimulants got no academic benefit, while underslept kids did. The mechanism involves circadian clock biology: true ADHD is heavily co-morbid with delayed sleep phase disorder (some studies put it as high as 80%), and chronic sleep debt produces the exact checklist of ADHD symptoms. His practical upshot: prioritizing sleep quality and circadian alignment should come before assuming a stimulant gap — and the Yerkes-Dodson arousal curve explains why stimulants harm performance in already-optimally-aroused individuals.

Dr. Sten Ekberg

Ekberg corrects a widely-cited claim from Robert Lustig's viral sugar lecture (25M+ views): the mechanism that fructose goes straight to the liver and can only be processed there is wrong based on a 2018 discovery. The small intestine actually processes fructose first — at low doses (~10–25g per sitting in humans, extrapolated from mouse data), the gut handles ~90% of fructose, converting it to glucose and lactate, with the liver only handling overflow. The dose and speed of consumption determine how much reaches the liver: liquid fructose on an empty stomach overwhelms the gut quickly (~10g threshold), while fructose consumed slowly with fiber raises the ceiling (~25g). Critically, fructose is still damaging for other reasons — it doesn't suppress ghrelin the way protein/fat do, doesn't trigger an insulin response that signals satiety, and brain imaging shows stronger cravings and preference for high-calorie immediate rewards after fructose vs. glucose consumption. The practical upshot: a 20oz soda (36g fructose) or a medium fast food milkshake (25g fructose) will stress the liver; a serving of berries (~2.4–2.7g per 100g) probably won't.

🧬 Health & Science

Fructose Mechanism Update — The Lustig Correction

Dr. Sten Ekberg's detailed breakdown (summarized above in YouTube section) is worth flagging as a standalone finding: the 2018 discovery that the small intestine — not the liver — is the primary site of fructose processing at low doses rewrites the standard mechanism cited by health professionals for 17 years. The dose-dependence is the actionable part: the gut saturates quickly with liquid sugar (threshold ~10g fructose, roughly half a can of soda), after which overflow goes to the liver and the old Lustig mechanism kicks in. The practical hierarchy for fructose load: berries are fine (2.4–2.7g/100g), a single can of Coke hits 21g, a 20oz bottle hits 36g. Fructose's satiety-suppression via ghrelin and its lack of insulin response remain valid mechanisms independent of the liver-first claim.

ADHD, Sleep, and Stimulants — Nick Norwitz's Cell Study Breakdown

The Cell study Norwitz covers (detailed above) is a meaningful finding worth flagging separately: fMRI data on ~12,000 children shows ADHD stimulants don't touch attention networks and instead act on arousal and reward systems — effectively mimicking the effect of better sleep in underslept children, with no benefit in well-rested children. The prevalence estimate that only 48% of children in the study were getting recommended sleep, combined with the heavy overlap between delayed sleep phase disorder and ADHD diagnoses (up to 80% in some studies), raises a real question about how much diagnosed ADHD is actually chronic circadian misalignment being managed pharmacologically. The Yerkes-Dodson arousal curve explains both paradoxes: why underperformers improve on stimulants and why already-optimal performers get worse.

Peter Attia — Keto Works, But Not How We Thought

Attia's updated position (detailed above) is worth noting as a clean example of the "strong convictions, loosely held" principle applied to nutrition: he spent 3 years in tight ketosis (average morning BHB of 1.8–2.0 mmol/L), felt great, but now believes the mechanism was satiety and spontaneous calorie reduction — not insulin suppression. The clinical implication he draws: if hyperinsulinemia is downstream of energy imbalance rather than a root cause, then targeting insulin without fixing energy balance won't resolve metabolic disease. This cuts against the strong insulin-model-of-obesity framing that drove much of early low-carb advocacy.

Ledger confirms spy chip implant behind wallet crypto thefts

Ledger has confirmed that hardware wallets purchased through compromised supply chains contained physical spy chips that exfiltrated seed phrases, leading to direct theft of funds. This is a supply chain attack on hardware security devices — the exact category of product people buy precisely because they don't trust software-level security — and has prompted Trezor to audit and reduce its reseller network. The implication: for anyone holding meaningful Bitcoin in cold storage, provenance of the physical device now matters as much as the device's software security model.
