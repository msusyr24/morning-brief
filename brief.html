Chesterton's Fence

Before removing a rule or system you don't understand, figure out why it was put there. Every rule was someone's solution to a problem. If you can't articulate the problem, you might be about to recreate it. Munger applies this to business reorganizations: understand the old structure first.

🎥 From People I Follow

Worth your time:

GTO Wizard

Tom Boss 21 makes a rigorous empirical case that late registration in MTTs is significantly +EV — not just in theory, but in real data. Using WSOP tournament scrapes and ICM modeling, he shows the starting stack appreciates 10–20% in value depending on how late you enter (e.g., with 30% of the field remaining, a $200 stack is worth ~$240). Triton data (8,000 bullets, 2025–2026) confirms the mechanism holds in practice: late entrants cashed at 19% vs. 15% for day-one entrants, and players outperformed their own earlier bullets when late-entering. One counterintuitive finding: late reg does NOT increase variance — the standard deviation of outcomes stays nearly identical, meaning your risk-adjusted ROI actually improves. The practical edge: you can shot-take bigger tournaments with a smaller bankroll. One important caveat — after 4 re-entries, ROI craters sharply ("minus 40%"), suggesting tilt explains mass rebuying. His five-bullet rule: if you've fired four bullets in a tournament, walk away.

Thomas DeLauer

Dr. John Chef (longevity physician) argues that the longevity space has become dominated by anxiety-inducing obsession with single surrogate data points — grip strength, VO2 max — at the expense of the actual levers. His core claim: VO2 max, despite being called "the single most powerful predictor of longevity" by many physicians, is actually just a proxy for cardiopulmonary output, and critically, it can be improved 30% in 6 weeks of HIIT, then lost just as fast. Skeletal muscle mass, by contrast, takes years to build and requires very little to maintain — which is exactly why it should receive disproportionate attention. The duality he emphasizes: growth/muscle health on one side, repair/recovery on the other, with metabolic health (especially fasting insulin) at the center. His practical critique of the content ecosystem: that busy non-expert viewers walk away thinking "I need to improve my grip strength" instead of addressing root causes like insulin resistance and muscle loss.

₿ Bitcoin & Macro

Citi to Debut Bitcoin Custody for Institutional Investors

Citibank is launching a "Custody+" service that allows institutional investors to hold Bitcoin alongside traditional assets on a single platform. This continues the trend of major TradFi institutions building Bitcoin infrastructure for institutional clients — following BlackRock's ETF and Fidelity's custody offering — and signals that Bitcoin's role as a portfolio asset is being normalized at the largest banks regardless of price action.

Bitcoin Magazine

Bitcoin's Wild Swings Go Quiet, Even as the Bears Won't Let Go

VanEck research shows Bitcoin's realized volatility has dampened significantly — a structural shift that, if sustained, strengthens the "digital real estate" and treasury reserve asset thesis (less volatility = more palatable for corporate and institutional balance sheets). BlackRock separately reiterated that Bitcoin's investment thesis as a global monetary alternative is intact despite price weakness this year.

37% of US Workers Saw Real Wage Declines 2021–2024

A University of Chicago working paper finds that over a third of US workers experienced real (inflation-adjusted) wage declines across this period despite nominal wage growth — the inflation tax hitting workers hardest at the bottom of the distribution. This is precisely the monetary debasement dynamic that the Bitcoin-as-hard-money thesis is built around: nominal gains masking real purchasing power destruction.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia Podcast #404 — Mental Health, Hormones, and Psychedelic Therapies

Dr. Linus Abrams argues that psychiatry has been organized around symptom reduction rather than restoration of full human function — a distinction with real treatment implications. He makes the case that hormones are a substantially underappreciated lever in psychiatric outcomes (beyond standard neurotransmitter models), and discusses where psychedelic therapies fit in this broader framework. Worth listening to if you find the conventional SSRI-dominant model of mental health incomplete.

Children's Lung Recovery in Ultra-Low Emission Zone — Real Data

Scientists describe being "stunned" by the speed and magnitude of lung function recovery in children living in a UK ultra-low emission zone — a rare natural experiment providing evidence that air quality improvements can reverse measurable physiological damage in developing lungs within a relatively short window. The mechanism is well-established (PM2.5 and NO2 impair lung development), but the recovery rate apparently exceeded expectations.

💡 Worth Reading

The Amazon Tax

Seth Godin's most-read piece of the day argues that Amazon has become a de facto tax on commerce — merchants pay to exist in its ecosystem, those costs are passed to consumers, and the platform extracts margin at every layer while providing the infrastructure everyone is now dependent on. The "tax" framing recontextualizes platform fees not as a service but as rent extraction with no competitive alternative.

Forget the Good Life, Try the Good Day

Massimo Pigliucci (Figs in Winter) argues that the Stoic focus on the present moment is practically superior to abstract life-goal thinking — that the day, not the life, is the unit we actually control. The piece applies this to modern productivity culture, where deferred meaning ("I'll be fulfilled when...") is the dominant failure mode. Concise and worth sitting with.

How to Mentally Rehearse Coping

Donald Robertson (lesson 2 of his Stoic Mind Miniseries) lays out the actual Stoic practice of negative visualization and pre-rehearsal of adversity — not as pessimism but as deliberate inoculation against emotional shock. The companion piece The Stoic Taxonomy of Anger is also worth reading: it maps exactly how the Stoics classified anger into proto-passions (involuntary physical responses), full passions (judgments we assent to), and voluntary behavior — a distinction that has real practical utility for emotional regulation.
