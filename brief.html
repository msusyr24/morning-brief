Nick Norwitz

Detailed breakdown of the "entourage effect" in caffeine — the idea that the same 100mg dose hits completely differently depending on what molecules accompany it. Key distinctions: coffee delivers free caffeine that peaks in blood in 30–45 minutes (fast, jittery); green tea and matcha include L-theanine, which promotes alpha-wave "relaxed alertness" and blunts the edge; yerba mate contains all three major methylxanthines (caffeine, theobromine, theophylline) for a slower, smoother multi-wave stimulus; guarana binds caffeine in tannins for extended release; dark chocolate's 3:1 theobromine-to-caffeine ratio means it's mostly a cardiovascular rather than CNS stimulant. A useful practical finding: Celsius contains EGCG and ginger root extract, which inhibit the enzymes (COMT and MAO) that break down adrenaline — meaning it may prolong stimulant effects beyond what the caffeine dose alone would suggest, which could explain why some people find it harsh. French press raises LDL 10–20 mg/dL more than filtered coffee due to diterpenes passing through the metal mesh.

Renaissance Periodization

Two sharp, brief training science points. First: rep ranges from ~5 to ~30 reps, taken close to failure, produce roughly equivalent hypertrophy — but heavy sets (5s) carry higher acute injury risk and beat up joints/connective tissue meaningfully more than moderate-rep work. Second: for people training 2–3x per week for ~30 minutes, full-body sessions every session is the optimal approach — body-part splits add complexity without benefit at that frequency and volume. RP also tested several plant-based protein products and found wide variance in actual protein quality: wheat gluten-based products (many "keto" sausages, lunch meats) have very low digestible protein — a labeled 14g serving may deliver only ~4–5g usable protein. Soy and pea protein sources (Impossible Burger, Smart Dogs, Abbott's Ground Beef) performed much better on protein quality per calorie. Non-animal whey (beta-lactoglobulin) was described as chemically identical to the most anabolic fraction of real whey.

Thomas DeLauer

Two videos worth noting. The fruit/fructose timing piece is genuinely mechanistic: AMPK (the cell's fat-synthesis brake) peaks mid-morning and hits its daily low in the evening — independent of meals, built into the cell's circadian clock. Liver glycogen status is the other key variable: depleted glycogen (post-fast, post-workout) routes fructose to energy; full glycogen routes it to fat via de novo lipogenesis. A rodent study found that shifting feeding to the active phase alone — same calories — reduced liver triglyceride accumulation by 50%. A third mechanism: fructose generates uric acid, and renal clearance of uric acid drops overnight, so evening fructose can raise nocturnal cortisol and fragment sleep. Practical takeaway: front-load fruit to late morning (after AMPK peaks), and if eating fruit at night, favor berries and tart cherry juice (quercetin inhibits uric acid production). The Laird Hamilton interview on fear is philosophically interesting — Hamilton's thesis is that fear is fundamentally about the unknown, and that subjecting yourself to the feared thing consistently takes its power away ("once you submit, you're not thinking about what it's going to do, you're thinking about what you're going to do").

An Austrian Economist Explains Why AI Will Make Everyone an Entrepreneur

Per Bylund argues that AI's predictive and execution power handles the operational side of business, but cannot substitute for human entrepreneurial vision — the identification of not-yet-existing market opportunities. His conclusion is that the economy shifts from employment (executing known tasks) toward entrepreneurship (identifying unknown ones), since AI automates the former but cannot originate the latter. A genuinely interesting Austrian-lens take on AI's economic effect, distinct from the usual labor displacement framing.

Dr. Sten Ekberg: Four Mechanisms Behind Waking at 3am

Ekberg lays out four distinct physiological causes for consistent 3am waking, each with a different upstream source. (1) Blood glucose crash: insulin resistance causes a delayed, excessive glucose drop ~5–6 hours after a high-carb/late dinner, triggering adrenaline and cortisol release that wakes you — solution is earlier, lower-carb dinner with more fat and protein. (2) Sleep apnea microarousals: soft palate muscle tone drops during deep sleep; specific oropharyngeal exercises can raise baseline tone enough to prevent airway collapse without CPAP. (3) Chronic mental stress elevates the HPA axis baseline; the natural cortisol rise beginning around 3am pushes an already-elevated baseline over the waking threshold — parasympathetic breathing training (4–5 second inhale, 5–6 second slow silent exhale, daily for 5–10 min) physically strengthens parasympathetic nerve nuclei over time. (4) A fourth mechanism (circadian-driven cortisol rhythm interaction with light sleep cycles) ties all three together anatomically.

It's Time to Investigate the AI Labs

Cal Newport argues for formal regulatory scrutiny of frontier AI labs — not the usual safety/alignment framing, but a more concrete concern about the societal and economic disruption being deployed without accountability structures. Separately, a leaked quote from a Simon Willison post (attributed to a security researcher) is striking: "To say that we were surprised at the jump and suddenness of the capabilities of our models when it came to 'cyber' or 'swarming' or 'message boards' or anything else related to the incidents is an understatement. Security posture takes time to develop." This suggests internal lab teams are themselves caught off-guard by capability jumps, which makes Newport's call for external oversight harder to dismiss.

The Past Is Not a Defendant — Massimo Pigliucci

Pigliucci argues that retrospective moral judgment of historical cultures is both lazy and unhelpful — the Stoic alternative is to learn from the past rather than condemn it, which requires actually understanding why people made the choices they did within their epistemic and material constraints. A short essay but a useful corrective to the common pattern of using history as a mirror for present-day virtue signaling rather than as a source of practical wisdom.

How to Cope Wisely with Anxiety — Donald Robertson

Part of Robertson's Stoic Mind miniseries, this installment applies Stoic cognitive techniques specifically to anxiety — distinguishing between what is genuinely under your control versus projected catastrophe, and using premeditation of adversity (premeditatio malorum) not to become pessimistic but to dissolve the unknown-threat quality that gives anxiety its power. Pairs well with the Laird Hamilton observation in DeLauer's video that fear is "the unknown" and subjecting yourself to feared situations consistently removes their grip.
