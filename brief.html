GTO Wizard

Tom Boss walks through a deep empirical case for late registration in MTTs, combining ICM theory with real WSOP and Triton data. The core finding: late-registering a starting stack when ~40-50% of the field remains boosts its ICM value by roughly 10-15%, and real tournament data from Triton (8,000 bullets) confirms this — players made more money on their second bullet than their first, even after controlling for skill by comparing each player against their own earlier entries. Critically, variance does not increase with late reg despite conventional wisdom; if anything, the risk-adjusted return improves, meaning you can shot-take larger fields with a smaller bankroll. The one clear exception: firing a 5th+ bullet typically signals tilt — the data shows ROI collapses past bullet four, supporting a hard "five bullet" stop rule.

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz summarizes a compelling twin study showing that greater explosive leg power at baseline predicted significantly less cognitive decline and larger gray matter brain volume over 12 years — even in identical twins, where the stronger sibling showed a directional reversal in cognitive trajectory. The mechanism runs through four pathways: (1) muscle contraction releases irisin, which crosses the blood-brain barrier and stimulates BDNF production in the hippocampus; (2) large muscle groups are the body's primary glucose sponge (~80% of insulin-stimulated uptake), improving metabolic health critical for brain function; (3) exercise improves cardiovascular fitness and cerebral blood flow; (4) exercise elevates NAD levels via NAMPT, supporting mitochondrial energy production in neurons. Practical minimum: 30 minutes of leg-focused resistance training twice per week (squats, deadlifts, lunges). He also notes that visceral fat specifically — not subcutaneous fat — is linked to faster brain shrinkage via inflammatory signaling and insulin resistance in the brain.

Anthony Chaffee MD

Chaffee argues that "fasting metabolism" is a misnomer — it's actually the body's primary metabolic state, not a starvation state. His claim: all animals, including herbivores (which break fiber down into fat and protein via gut bacteria), run primarily on fat and protein. Therefore, eating 5,000 calories of ribeye keeps you in the same metabolic state as fasting; you don't need OMAD or time-restricted eating to get the benefits — you just need to keep insulin low by avoiding carbohydrates. Fasting helps only insofar as it lets insulin clear.

Paul Saladino MD

Saladino delivers a detailed breakdown of nicotine's history and the science behind its perceived cognitive benefits. Key claim: according to researcher Paul Newhouse at Vanderbilt (30 years of nicotine research), nicotine does NOT enhance cognition in healthy individuals — it only appears to help when baseline cognition is already impaired (e.g., mild dementia). The widely cited 2010 Hushman meta-analysis showing benefits largely used smokers deprived of nicotine for 4 hours, then given a patch — a confounded design. The tobacco industry's core trick: converting nicotine to its freebase form via diammonium phosphate (the same process as cocaine → crack), dramatically accelerating blood-brain barrier crossing and addictive potential. Philip Morris now owns Zyn via its $16B acquisition of Swedish Match in 2022, applying the same brand-building playbook. Bottom line: nicotine in a healthy person likely does nothing beneficial and creates dependence; the nootropic narrative is largely industry-driven.

🧬 Health & Science

Leg Power → Brain Volume: The Twin Study

Nick Norwitz covered this in depth (see YouTube section above), but the underlying study deserves a standalone flag. Researchers tracked 324 people over a decade and separately studied twins: the twin with greater explosive leg power at baseline showed significantly less cognitive decline and larger gray matter volume on MRI 12 years later. The stronger twin in identical pairs showed a directional reversal — their cognition improved while the weaker twin's declined. The mechanistic chain: muscle contraction → irisin release → crosses blood-brain barrier → stimulates BDNF in hippocampus → neurogenesis and synaptogenesis. Leg muscles matter disproportionately because they're the largest muscle groups, releasing proportionally more irisin per contraction than smaller muscles like biceps.

Gum Recession: Often Worsened by the Habits Meant to Prevent It

Peter Attia's practice flagged that gum recession is frequently silent until significant tissue loss has occurred, and that aggressive brushing technique is one of the most common causes — meaning well-intentioned oral hygiene can accelerate the condition. The implication is that technique (soft bristles, gentle circular motion) matters more than intensity, and that most people won't notice recession until it's already meaningful.

Visceral Fat Shrinks the Brain via Inflammation

Nick Norwitz (citing randomized trial data) notes that visceral fat — not subcutaneous fat — is specifically linked to faster brain shrinkage. The mechanism: visceral fat acts as an endocrine organ secreting inflammatory cytokines that promote neuroinflammation, insulin resistance, and energy depletion in the brain. You can have visible abs and still carry dangerous levels of visceral fat; DEXA scans routinely show this in people at 10-13% body fat.

How to Mentally Rehearse Coping

Donald Robertson (one of the best Stoic practitioners writing today) covers the Stoic technique of negative visualization as active rehearsal — not just contemplating adversity but mentally walking through your response to it. This is the practical cognitive skill underlying Stoic equanimity: you pre-commit to how you'll act before the stressor arrives, so you're not deciding under pressure. Worth pairing with his companion piece on the taxonomy of anger.

The Stoic Taxonomy of Anger

Robertson maps how the Stoics classified anger — distinguishing between the involuntary pre-emotion (proto-passion, unavoidable), the moment of assent (where you can intervene), and full-blown anger (a failure of judgment). This framework is practically useful: it locates the point of intervention not at suppression but at the moment you choose whether to endorse the initial reaction.

One Night in Uzbekistan: Why Was This One Data Point So Influential?

A real replication failure story: researchers couldn't reproduce a published finding and traced the entire effect to a single anomalous data point from Uzbekistan. This is a useful case study in how outlier sensitivity, model fragility, and the absence of robustness checks can let a spurious result survive peer review. Good mental model fuel for evaluating any social science finding.
