GTO Lab (Nick Petrangelo, Daniel Dvoress & Axel Hallay)

The core thesis: mystery bounty tournaments are not "regular poker with slightly wider calls" — they're a structurally different game, and most players massively underadjust. The key metric is the KO-to-stack ratio (bounty value ÷ current stack), which should drive all pre- and post-flop strategy. At early stages with 1,000 players remaining, ICM is essentially irrelevant, chips are worth the least relative to the prize pool, and the bounty is at its most valuable — meaning players should be VPIPing close to 100% from many positions, something almost no one does. The common mistake runs in one direction: players anchored to vanilla tournament thinking play too tight and forfeit EV that's sitting on the table. GTO Lab built a 240+ sim library with tools that separate dollar EV into prize-pool EV (green) vs. KO EV (yellow), so you can see when a call is pure bounty-justified and negative for tournament equity — which completely reframes whether to make it.

Video: Mystery Bounty Is a Completely Different Game | Nick Petrangelo, Daniel Dvoress & Axel Hallay

Jared Alderman (with Uri Peleg)

Uri Peleg's central argument: the question "am I balanced?" is the wrong question. The correct question is "can my opponent detect if I'm unbalanced?" — and the practical answer is almost never yes. This means you can systematically over-bluff some board textures and under-bluff others (e.g., over-bluff ace-high boards, under-bluff king-high boards) and remain unexploitable in practice, because no opponent has the sample size or precision to isolate it. The chess analogy lands well: GTO is two players setting up invisible chessboards; exploitative play is making an informed guess about your opponent's board and countering it. Jared's randomized-sizing idea (using size randomization as a form of hidden information rather than frequency randomization) generates the interesting insight that checking becomes much harder to read if your opponent knows you sometimes bet any size including all-in — but Uri grounds it: practically, mastering a one-size game tree per spot is already elite-level, and multiple sizes add enormous tree complexity that almost no one can execute.

Video: "Poker Doesn't Work Without Exploits" - Uri Peleg

Lex Fridman (with Steven Brunton, Ankur Moitra & Alex)

This is a genuine "emergency episode" triggered by OpenAI releasing proofs of ~700 results including progress toward the Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis, the Unique Games Conjecture, and improvements to Fast Fourier Transform — results that, if done by humans, would be instant Fields Medals. The panel's honest assessment: the AI is operating in the "semigroup" of known mathematical moves rather than the "convex hull" (not derogatory — the semigroup can reach far further than humans have explored), combining known techniques in sequences no human would have patience to try. No genuinely new mathematical ideas yet — but the cascade of known moves is long enough to be "shocking." The most interesting tension: Terence Tao's concern that graduate students lose the ability to explore a problem richly once a solution exists (you can't unhear a spoiler), while others argue knowing a solution is possible is itself a massive accelerant. The "doorman fallacy" framing is worth keeping: reducing a mathematician's job to "proving theorems" misses what they actually do — building theories, asking the right questions, creating beautiful structures that generalize. The panel agrees asking the right questions will become the scarce human skill.

Video: AI Crisis in Math: Navier-Stokes, Riemann Hypothesis & Complexity Theory | Lex Fridman Podcast #503

Peter Attia (with Jeff Cavaliere)

Cavaliere's framework on reps in reserve (RIR): 0 RIR = mechanical failure, 1 RIR = one grinding rep left, 2–3 RIR = still hard but no outside observer notices, 4+ RIR = junk volume. His argument is that most people systematically underestimate what they can do by 1–3 reps, which means their entire calibration is off — if you don't truly know what 0 feels like, every other number is a guess. The practical prescription: train to good-form failure (not ugly-rep failure, not psychological failure) on most sets, then strip out the volume you no longer need. The key distinction is psychological failure vs. mechanical failure — discomfort aversion is not the same as muscular incapacity, and the signal for hypertrophy requires getting past the former to reach the latter.

🧬 Health & Science

Reps in Reserve: What "Failure" Actually Means (Peter Attia / Jeff Cavaliere)

The practical upshot of Cavaliere's RIR framework is that most trainees are running junk volume without knowing it because they've never actually reached 0 RIR — true mechanical failure — and therefore have no calibrated reference point. He distinguishes three failure types: psychological failure (discomfort aversion, not a real limit), form failure (acceptable if the joint isn't hurting), and mechanical failure (the actual training signal). The fix: reach good-form mechanical failure more often, then reduce total set count — you get more hypertrophic signal with less joint wear and time.

Brown Fat, Metabolic Health, and the LDL Paradox (Nick Norwitz)

A Nature study (n > 100,000) found people with more brown adipose tissue had 22–48% lower odds of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, depression, and anxiety. Brown fat is metabolically active because it's dense with mitochondria — it burns rather than stores. The study identified losartan (a common ARB blood pressure medication) as one pharmacological activator. The buried finding Norwitz flags: higher brown fat was associated with better cardiometabolic outcomes but also higher LDL — a data point that cuts against simple LDL-as-risk-factor narratives and is worth sitting with.

Alternate-Day Caloric Restriction for Body Composition (Thomas DeLauer)

DeLauer's protocol for rapid fat loss centers on alternating high-calorie days (~3,000 kcal) with aggressive low-calorie days (~1,200 kcal), manipulating primarily fat intake while keeping protein constant (~1g per lb of lean mass) on both days — this is the evidence-based "intermittent caloric restriction" model, which appears to be more metabolically protective than continuous restriction of the same magnitude. He also cites a 2023 exercise physiology study suggesting spot reduction is possible when combining targeted resistance work with cardio simultaneously (the resistance mobilizes local fat, cardio oxidizes it) — though he appropriately notes it's one unreplicated study. The supplement stack he describes (yohimbine 15–20mg on a 2-on/1-off cycle, rudaarpine to accelerate caffeine clearance, tryptophan + theanine for sleep) is more extreme than everyday use warrants, but the alternating calorie cycling principle is well-grounded.

How to Use Rational Emotive Imagery

Donald Robertson walks through a specific Stoic/REBT mental rehearsal technique: vividly imagining a negative scenario, allowing yourself to feel the disturbed emotion, then actively working to shift the emotion (not suppress it) toward something more rational — practicing this daily until the new response becomes automatic. This is the practical side of Stoic premeditation (premeditatio malorum) combined with Ellis's REBT, and it's more actionable than most philosophy-adjacent content. Worth reading alongside Epictetus's Handbook #41, which Robertson also covers this week.

AI Is Forcing Us to Pay Off 40 Years of Technical Debt

IEEE Spectrum piece arguing that AI systems are now surfacing and exploiting vulnerabilities in legacy codebases — particularly ancient attack surfaces like PHP shells — that have been ignored for decades because automated tools couldn't find them and humans didn't have the time. The argument is that AI-driven security research is compressing a 40-year debt reckoning into a very short window, and organizations that haven't modernized their stacks are suddenly extremely exposed. Pairs well with the OpenAI kernel patching item above.

Progress on Provable Inference for Large Language Models

Anthropic research on making LLM outputs formally verifiable — i.e., being able to prove properties about what a model will or won't output under certain conditions. This is early-stage but points toward a future where AI outputs in high-stakes domains (medical, legal, security) can carry formal guarantees rather than probabilistic assurances. The connection to the OpenAI kernel work is direct: if you're using AI to generate security patches, knowing the patch has provable properties matters.
