🧬 Health & Science

Early-Life Stress Leaves a 'Scar' Inside Brain Cells (Mouse Study)

Washington University researchers found that early-life stress leaves detectable molecular changes inside individual brain cells in mice — described as a "scar" at the cellular level, not just behavioral or hormonal. The implication is that the effects of adversity during development may be encoded more durably than previously understood, potentially informing future work on resilience and trauma. This is mouse-model research, so human applicability is preliminary, but the mechanism (intracellular rather than just synaptic or endocrine) is a meaningful refinement.

Ben Felix on the Compounding Logic of Health Investment

Buried in the YouTube summary above but worth flagging separately as a health data point: a 2018 study found that Americans maintaining five basic lifestyle factors (no smoking, healthy BMI, 30+ min/day moderate exercise, moderate alcohol, high diet quality) live 12–14 years longer than those who don't. Felix's physician reviewers flagged VO2 max as the single most actionable longevity lever — the JAMA 2018 study shows the mortality risk of low cardiorespiratory fitness is equal to or greater than smoking. On nutrition: his conclusion (backed by his MD co-hosts) is that the evidence is genuinely uncertain beyond a few basics — avoid blood sugar spikes, eat protein, eat fiber, avoid highly processed food — and "the best diet is the one you can stick to."

💡 Worth Reading

AI Boosted Homework Scores, Then Exam Scores Dropped

The Economist covers a study showing that students who used AI assistance saw their homework grades improve — but their performance on exams (where AI wasn't available) declined. This is a clean natural experiment on the difference between AI as a crutch vs. AI as a tutor, and it has obvious implications for how learning and retention actually work when you can always offload cognitive effort. The key question it raises: does using AI for a task help you learn it, or just help you complete it?

I'm Becoming AI-Blind

A well-timed essay on a real perceptual shift: the author describes increasingly tuning out AI-generated content on instinct — not because it's always bad, but because the aesthetic and epistemic signature of AI output has become recognizable enough to trigger automatic dismissal. This is the reader-side equivalent of ad-blindness, and it's worth sitting with as someone who consumes a lot of written content. The interesting downstream question is what this does to trust in written communication generally.

Felony Charges for Citizen Deleting Phone Data at US Border

A U.S. citizen is facing felony charges for deleting data from their phone during a border search. The legal theory being used — that deleting your own data constitutes obstruction — has significant implications for digital privacy and the practical rights of anyone crossing a U.S. border.
