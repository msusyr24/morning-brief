Peter Attia

Two pieces from Attia this period. On Zone 2: the central argument is that Zone 2 training is a balancing act between accumulated volume and fatigue — more isn't always better if recovery is compromised, and the frame should be "sustainable dose" rather than "maximum dose." On metabolic liver health (AMA preview): Attia frames the liver as the canary in the coal mine for systemic metabolic dysfunction — fatty liver disease (now called MASLD) affects an estimated 38% of the world's adult population and the leading cause of death in those patients is cardiovascular disease, not liver failure. Andrew Huberman (ft. Dr. Chris Palmer)

Palmer's thesis: nutrition — specifically ketogenic diets — can function as a serious treatment for severe psychiatric illness, not just mood optimization. His most striking case: a 33-year-old man with schizoaffective disorder who had failed 17 medications and had daily hallucinations and paranoid delusions. Within 6–8 weeks of a ketogenic diet, his psychotic symptoms spontaneously resolved; he later completed a certificate program, performed improv in front of a live audience, and moved out of his father's home. Palmer's mechanistic claim: the brain benefits appear tied to ketosis specifically, not just carb reduction — patients who tried the diet without achieving measurable ketones typically didn't respond clinically. His target blood ketone levels: >0.8 mmol for depression, >1.5 mmol for psychotic and bipolar disorders. He's careful to note this doesn't replace medication and that coming off psychiatric meds requires professional supervision.

🧬 Health & Science

Clonal Hematopoiesis: The Cardiovascular Risk Factor Nobody Talks About

Nick Norwitz flagged an emerging risk factor: clonal hematopoiesis, where mutant immune cells generated in bone marrow accumulate with age, travel to arterial tissue, and drive cardiovascular inflammation — contributing to heart attack risk independent of cholesterol. It's described as "a cousin of cancer" because the mutant cells can eventually crowd out healthy cells. Two free interventions with evidence behind them: regular exercise and quality sleep. Worth tracking as this enters mainstream cardiology discussion.

Nick Norwitz

Selective Hepatic Insulin Resistance: Why Fatty Liver Accelerates Everything Else

Peter Attia's AMA on liver health contains a mechanistically important insight often missed in popular health content: insulin resistance doesn't hit the liver uniformly. The liver loses the "stop releasing glucose" signal before it loses the "make new fat" signal — so it continues dumping glucose into an already-high-sugar bloodstream while the pancreas responds with more insulin, which keeps driving fat production. This is why metabolic liver disease directly amplifies atherosclerosis risk: the liver overproduces ApoB-containing particles (LDL, VLDL) throughout this process. Steatosis (stage 2) is largely reversible; fibrosis (stage 4) largely is not — making early detection critical.

Ketogenic Diet as Psychiatric Treatment: Specific Targets and Cases

From the Huberman/Palmer conversation: Palmer's clinical protocol uses blood ketone levels as the dosing metric — >0.8 mmol for depression, >1.5 mmol for psychotic and bipolar disorders — and he reports that patients who attempted the diet without achieving measurable ketosis typically showed no clinical benefit. His most striking data point is the schizoaffective case (17 prior medication failures, daily hallucinations) where symptoms resolved within 6–8 weeks of sustained ketosis. Palmer also notes that the worst foods metabolically — and likely psychiatrically — are those high in both sugar and fat simultaneously (ultra-processed foods), and that even partial dietary improvements (eliminating junk food) can meaningfully move mood disorder outcomes.

Cortisol → T3 Suppression: The Stress-Metabolism Link with Human Data

Thomas DeLauer summarizes a JRSM Cardiovascular Disease study comparing cardiovascular disease patients to healthy controls: nearly 40% of CVD patients had elevated cortisol and ~38% had reduced active thyroid hormone (T3). The mechanism: chronic cortisol elevation downregulates the enzymes that convert inactive T4 to active T3, effectively turning down the metabolic volume knob — producing fatigue that doesn't match sleep, cold intolerance, insulin resistance, and brain fog, even when standard TSH labs look normal (TSH is often the last marker to shift, while T3 is the first signal the body actually feels). Practical implication: if fat loss feels stuck despite effort, the problem may be hormonal downregulation, not caloric math.

Benjamin Franklin's Alter Egos Gave Him the Most Freedom

Franklin used pen names and fictional personas — most famously "Poor Richard" but also others — not just for anonymity but as a tool for thinking and writing that he couldn't do under his own name. The piece makes a subtle point about how persona-adoption enables intellectual risk-taking, and how some of his most honest observations about human nature came through characters rather than as "Benjamin Franklin."
