Alderman: after a big loss, grieve the lost chips before acting

Alderman argues that tilt after losing a big pot comes from part of the mind still behaving as if you have the chips you lost. Pretending you don't care about winning, or just focusing on your next decision, leaves the pain unprocessed. His alternative is to want to win fully while consciously accepting the outcome is out of your control. When you lose the pot, let yourself feel the drop in resources, which updates your model of the situation. Then you can choose actions suited to your real stack.

  • Two questions: what's happening, and what's needed? Answer them in that order.
  • While you are still updating, fold marginal opens and take a breather instead of taking risks.
  • When much has changed, slow down rather than speed up.

Peter Attia

Attia: sensory loss is a modifiable dementia risk under the 'shrinking world' hypothesis

Attia frames dementia as partly a problem of degraded sensory input. Untreated hearing loss, cataracts, poor balance and neuropathy lead people to avoid activity, which means less novelty, movement and conversation. The Lancet Commission's 2024 report says up to 45% of dementia cases could be prevented through modifiable factors. Of those, 9% come from hearing or vision loss and 7% from inactivity and social isolation. He calls the hypothesis unproven and incomplete, since it doesn't address amyloid, pTau or alpha-synuclein. He sees it as one of the few dementia areas where people can act now. The piece covers hearing, sight, balance and proprioception, and early evidence on interoception.

  • Lancet 2024: up to 45% of dementia cases potentially preventable
  • Sensory loss accounts for 9%, inactivity and isolation for 7%

◎Mind & Philosophy

Figs in Winter (Pigliucci)

Pigliucci: the Stoic 'prohairesis' maps onto the brain's executive function

Pigliucci argues that the Stoic idea of prohairesis, our capacity for choice, corresponds to what neuroscientists call executive function. This is the ability to resist a cutting reply, hold a thought while gathering facts, or change plans mid-stream. As a physicalist, he thinks it is obvious why natural selection favored conscious awareness. He says the Stoics were wrong about where it sits anatomically. The rest of the piece is paywalled.

Daily Stoic reading · Delay before acting on impulse

If you are dazzled by the semblance of any promised pleasure, guard yourself against being bewildered by it; but let the affair wait your leisure, and procure yourself some delay. Then bring to your mind both points of time—that in which you shall enjoy the pleasure, and that in which you will repent and reproach yourself, after you have enjoyed it—and set before you, in opposition to these, how you will rejoice and applaud yourself if you abstain. And even though it should appear to you a seasonable gratification, take heed that its enticements and allurements and seductions may not subdue you, but set in opposition to this how much better it is to be conscious of having gained so great a victory.

— Epictetus, Enchiridion

When a tempting option looks attractive, do not decide on the spot; buy yourself time, then picture both the moment of enjoying it and the later regret, and weigh those against the satisfaction of staying disciplined. For a poker player this is the standard guard against chasing a loss, jamming a marginal spot after a bad beat, or taking a shot at a bigger game because the result feels due; the pull of the immediate gain is strongest exactly when the decision is worst.

Mental model · Psychology

Contrast Misreaction

We judge things relative to recent comparisons, not absolute values. A $2,000 suit feels cheap after you've committed to a $30,000 car. Real estate agents show you a worse house first. Munger: 'If you're selling something, show the dud first.' If you're buying, beware of contrast-engineered comparisons.
