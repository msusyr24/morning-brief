Jared Alderman

Jared's conversation with high-stakes heads-up player Uri Peleg gets into one of the most underappreciated ideas in poker: your opponent's uncertainty about your game tree is itself a strategic resource. Uri's key point — "it only matters if I bluff too much or too little if my opponent calls too much or too little" — reframes balance as a practical, not purely theoretical, concern. He argues you can over-bluff certain boards and under-bluff others globally without being exploited, because there's no practical way for opponents to detect this pattern. They also explore the chess-as-hidden-chessboard analogy: GTO poker is setting up your pieces optimally given your opponent doesn't know your board, while exploitative poker is guessing how he's set up his. Uri notes that even top nosebleed players lack the game tree mapped out at a basic one-size level on the river, making the randomized-sizing idea Jared pitches theoretically interesting but practically secondary to mastering simpler structures deeply.

GTO Lab

Nikki P and Danny D walk through their Mystery Bounty library with specialist coach Axel, who argues the single biggest leak in mystery bounty tournaments is playing too tight at bounty onset — players are so conditioned by vanilla MTT strategy that they under-adjust even when they know they should adjust more. The key analytical tool is the KO-to-stack ratio (bounty value divided by current stack), which Axel describes as the "golden ratio" that drives both preflop and postflop strategy. The library contains 240+ sims covering different stack depths, player counts, and bounty-to-stack ratios; a built-in calculator shows prize-pool EV vs. knockout EV separately so you can see when a call is negative in the regular tournament but profitable once the bounty is factored in. Axel's bottom line: in early bounty stages with short stacks, you're essentially playing a different game — some hands VPIP 100% from early position that you'd never touch in a regular MTT.

Lex Fridman

An emergency-format episode assembling three mathematicians to process OpenAI's release of claimed proofs on Navier-Stokes, a quasi-Riemann hypothesis result, the unique games conjecture, and hundreds of other problems. The guests resist calling this "solving math" and instead argue the AI is operating within the "semigroup generated by known moves" — not the derogatory "convex hull" (linear combinations of existing ideas), but a much larger space of what those moves can reach when composed at scale and breadth no human can match. The panel introduces the "doorman fallacy" as a framework: reducing a mathematician's job to "proving theorems" misses what they actually do (asking the right questions, building new conceptual structures, mentoring, curating what matters). The most sobering point: Terence Tao wrote that "one cannot explore a problem as profitably once aware of an existing solution," and the panelists acknowledge graduate students face a genuine crisis of meaning and professional blueprint — the old model of "take a problem people care about, improve it, get a paper" has been destabilized. On the optimistic side: AI has not yet generated a genuinely new mathematical definition or concept — it's combining, not extending the balloon's boundary.

ModernWisdom (Rob Kurzban)

Evolutionary psychologist Rob Kurzban's core framework: emotions are measurement-and-motivation systems — they measure something adaptively relevant (glucose level, proximity of a threat, social comparison) and then move you toward action. The reason feelings have to feel like something, rather than just processing invisibly like a thermostat, is that organisms with many competing drives need a common currency to arbitrate which priority wins. He extends this to explain why modern emotional mismatch is so pervasive: envy systems calibrated for small tribes get overwhelmed by Instagram; road rage evolved for people you'd see again but fires at strangers you'll never encounter. His taxonomy covers five kingdoms of emotion, and he notes that for many basic feelings — yawning, itching — the research base is embarrassingly thin.

Peter Attia on Rapamycin: What He Actually Doesn't Know

mTOR inhibition extends lifespan in all four standard eukaryotic models (yeast, flies, worms, rodents) — as strong a signal as exists in longevity biology. But Attia is candid that for humans, every practical question — dose, frequency, duration, cycling, timing of initiation — is genuinely unknown. The mouse studies used continuous dosing, which is different from the intermittent human protocols being tried. Matt Kaeberlein's dog study may eventually provide more relevant data. Attia's honest conclusion: he doesn't know if his once-weekly protocol clears the benefit/risk threshold, which is a notably humble stance for someone who has been one of rapamycin's highest-profile proponents.

Nick Norwitz: Mouthwash Destroys Beet-Derived Nitric Oxide Benefits

The mechanism: dietary nitrate (beets are the richest source) → oral bacteria convert to nitrite → converted to nitric oxide → vasodilation and improved blood flow. At ~540mg dietary nitrate (~1 cup beetroot), controlled human trials show improved athletic performance. The practical catch: antibacterial mouthwash kills the oral bacteria that initiate the nitrate→nitrite step, eliminating the benefit and, in some studies, measurably raising blood pressure. This is a specific, actionable interaction most athletes using beet-based supplements would be unaware of.

YouTube

Renaissance Periodization: Creatine Water Weight Is Intramuscular, Not Subcutaneous

The common complaint that creatine causes a "watery" look is largely wrong: creatine pulls water into the muscle cell itself (intramuscular), drawing from the extracellular compartment — not into subcutaneous space that creates the soft, undefined look people worry about. Up to 5 lbs of intramuscular water gain visually approximates 5 lbs of actual muscle. Separately: a transient cortisol spike during training is a positive signal, not something to suppress — studies tracking hormone levels pre/post session found the highest cortisol responders gained the most muscle over 12–16 week programs, because cortisol is a proxy for training intensity.

Massimo Pigliucci's series on Epictetus reaches Discourses 1.18, where Epictetus makes a Stoic case against capital punishment rooted in the distinction between what is "up to us" and what isn't — wrongdoers act from false judgments about what is good, which they cannot fully control, making punishment-as-retribution philosophically incoherent from a Stoic view. How to Use Rational Emotive Imagery

Robertson explains a daily mental rehearsal technique from REBT (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy): vividly imagine a distressing scenario, notice the unhealthy emotion that arises, then work to replace it with a healthier (but still realistic) emotion while holding the same image. The goal is to practice the emotional response you want until it becomes automatic — a Stoic-adjacent training method with a clinical backing that goes beyond simple positive visualization.
