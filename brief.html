Modern Wisdom (Chris Williamson)

A standout solo essay on obsession vs. discipline vs. motivation: Williamson argues discipline is "friction accepted" (high cost, reliable), motivation is "friction reduced" (unreliable, mood-dependent), and obsession is "friction inverted" — you can't avoid the work. His key insight is that obsession is a non-renewable fuel source that, when surrendered to fully, fossilizes into identity and becomes what looks like discipline from the outside. The practical implication: if you're currently obsessed with something positive, don't moderate it into respectability — let it take over, because by the time it fades, the rails of habit and identity are already laid. He also has a sharp short on the "elimination of alternatives" as the real mechanism of focus (Kobe's "does this make me a better basketball player?" filter), and a counterintuitive note that setting the bar unrealistically high does NOT increase performance — workload capacity doesn't expand like a stomach to fit more.

Thomas DeLauer

Solid deep-dive on sleep deprivation and visceral fat accumulation, anchored in a Mayo Clinic study showing that sleep restriction caused ~1 lb of weight gain, but CT scans revealed an 11% increase in visceral fat specifically. The mechanism: poor sleep elevates cortisol into the evening (preventing core temperature drop needed for sleep onset), raises ghrelin 28% and drops leptin 18% after just two nights of 4-hour sleep, and selectively increases appetite for calorie-dense carbs by 33–45% — mostly in the evening. A single night of partial sleep deprivation induces insulin resistance across multiple pathways; six nights of 4-hour sleep reduced glucose tolerance by up to 40%. His nutrient stack: glycine (3g before bed) to drop core temperature, 28mg Affron saffron to boost endogenous melatonin, L-theanine (200–400mg) which reduced salivary cortisol by 42% in one RCT, and magnesium threonate (crosses the blood-brain barrier; reversed cognitive age ~9 years in one Alzheimer's journal RCT).

🧬 Health & Science

Zero Coronary Calcium Does Not Mean Zero Cardiovascular Risk

Peter Attia's team covers an important clinical nuance: a coronary artery calcium (CAC) score of zero is prognostically useful, but should not be used to dismiss sustained LDL-C elevation in young adults. The core argument is that CAC scoring detects calcified plaque only — non-calcified (soft) plaque, which is both more prevalent in younger people and more rupture-prone, is invisible to the test. A zero score provides false reassurance that can lead clinicians and patients to ignore LDL trajectories that matter over 20–30 year horizons.
