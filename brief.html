Worth your time:

GTO Wizard

Solid exploitative strategy breakdown for PLO against loose-passive players. The core insight: recreational players don't differentiate between a half-pot bet and a 2/3-pot bet — they decide based on hand strength and whether they can improve, not pot odds. So sizing down with strong hands (as most players instinctively do) just gives them a better price without changing their calling range. The solver confirms this: against a node-locked loose-passive strategy, GTO shifts from mixed small/large sizing to almost exclusively large sizing with value hands, and actually reduces betting frequency on bluffs (no need to protect the betting range when villain isn't adjusting). Practical upshot: on a K97 flop with a set, bet large on flop, continue large on turn, and on rivers where you block villain's calling range, target the infrequent strong hands with large sizing rather than sizing down hoping to get called by nothing.

Jared Alderman

Alderman and a co-host dissect a live 1/3 hand where villain folds A8 two-pair on a turn minraise — the main thesis being that live players consistently underestimate the size of exploits they're making by "making dead reads." The hand in question had a theoretical folding cost of ~$50 EV. The broader point: live players justify not studying by claiming superior reads, but that heuristic breaks down when you're still getting the right price to call even if the read is correct (e.g., set of threes) because you have boat outs and blocking equity. The key distinction Alderman draws: online crushers have internalized pot odds as non-negotiable math; live players treat it as something to override with a story.

Angela Duckworth (Modern Wisdom)

Duckworth's new book argues that "try harder" is an incomplete heuristic — the real finding from behavioral science is that highly disciplined people aren't using more willpower, they're designing situations that require less of it (laying out gym clothes the night before, removing friction). The more interesting meta-point Chris Williamson surfaces: self-help advice has a selection effect — "advice hyperresponders" already grinding themselves into the ground will hear "try harder" and over-apply it, while the people who actually need it are impervious. Duckworth's correction: when effort isn't paying off, the instinct to try harder can be actively wrong; the situation itself may need changing, not the effort level. Her example of laying out gym clothes is trivial-sounding but backed by the principle that visual cues and environmental design drive behavior more than conscious will.

🧬 Health & Science

Myostatin Inhibitors: Larger But Weaker Muscles

Peter Attia covers an emerging paradox in drugs targeting myostatin (a protein that limits muscle growth): they reliably increase lean mass, but fail to produce commensurate strength gains. The mechanism appears to be that myostatin inhibition drives muscle fiber hypertrophy without the corresponding neural and contractile adaptations that underlie functional strength. This is a meaningful cautionary note for anyone tracking the GLP-1/muscle-preservation space — mass and strength are not the same variable, and drugs that optimize one may not move the other.

Peter Attia MD

Pediatric Type 2 Diabetes: 10-Fold Increase (2002–2023)

Data from the Colorado Diabetes Registry: incidence of T2D in kids aged 10–19 went from 2.37 per 100,000 in 2002 to 22.3 per 100,000 in 2023 — nearly a 10x increase, and the rate of increase itself accelerated from 2.9%/year (2002–2013) to 4.7%/year (2014–2023). Ken Berry's clinical argument: the standard pediatric screening protocol (fasting glucose + HbA1c) misses early hyperinsulinemia by years. A healthy child's fasting insulin should be under 5 μU/mL pre-puberty, rising to ~11-14 during puberty; anything above 13 in a child signals hyperinsulinemia that predates A1C elevation by potentially years. His call to action: demand a fasting insulin test at the same time as A1c, and cut added sugar and refined carbohydrates before the A1C crosses into pre-diabetes territory.

Ken Berry MD

Fermented Foods vs. Fiber: Stanford RCT Result

Nick Norwitz summarizes a landmark Stanford randomized human trial: a high-fermented-food diet (up to 6 servings/day of kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, yogurt, natto) produced consistent reductions in inflammatory markers and increases in microbiome diversity across subjects, while a high-fiber diet produced heterogeneous results — some subjects improved, others saw inflammatory markers increase. The practical implication: if you're adding one intervention for gut health, fermented foods have a more reliable signal than fiber alone, and the response to fiber may depend on existing microbiome composition in ways that are hard to predict without testing.

💡 Worth Reading

We're Going to Need Default Hard Budget Caps on Pretty Much Everything

Simon Willison argues that as AI agents and agentic workflows scale, the absence of hard spending caps on API-based services is a systemic risk — not just a billing inconvenience. The case: agents running amok (whether through bugs, prompt injection, or user error) can generate enormous costs before any human notices, and soft limits or warnings don't cut it. He calls for hard stops that return errors rather than continue spending, and argues this should be a default, not an opt-in feature. A narrow piece but the underlying principle — that agentic systems need circuit breakers built in, not bolted on — applies broadly to any infrastructure that charges by usage.

Reading Greek, Missing the Point (Parts I & II) — Massimo Pigliucci

Pigliucci takes on Mary Beard and Emily Wilson — two celebrated classicists — arguing that deep knowledge of the Greek text does not translate into philosophical understanding of Stoicism. The distinction he draws: knowing what a Stoic philosopher wrote and knowing what the philosophy demands of you are entirely different projects. Parts I and II together make the case that academic classics training can actually be an obstacle to living the philosophy, by substituting textual mastery for the harder work of appropriating the ideas. Worth reading if you follow his Figs in Winter newsletter — this is him at his sharpest on the theory/practice gap in Stoic study.

How to Find Strength in Your Own Character — Donald Robertson

Lesson 4 of Robertson's Stoic Mind Miniseries, focused on the Stoic practice of drawing resilience from one's own character rather than external circumstances. Robertson's core argument (consistent across his work) is that the Stoics didn't preach passive acceptance — they trained a specific cognitive skill of identifying what is genuinely yours (judgment, response, character) versus what isn't (outcomes, reputation, others' behavior), and leaning into the former under pressure.
