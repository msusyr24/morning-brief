Thomas DeLauer

Deep dive on allulose with specific human RCT data. Key findings: (1) At 10g alongside 50g sugar, insulin was 33% lower within 30 minutes (Journal of Functional Foods, n=14); (2) In a 12-week RCT (n=121), the high-allulose group (7g twice daily) showed significant reductions in BMI, belly fat, and subcutaneous fat vs. sucralose control at identical caloric intake; (3) A single dose shifted substrate oxidation measurably toward fat-burning and away from carb-burning. Three mechanisms: GLUT2 receptor competitive blocking (reduces intestinal glucose absorption), hepatic glucokinase translocation (liver soaks up more blood glucose into glycogen), and GLP-1 stimulation. Practical dosing: 5-10g with a carb-containing meal to blunt glucose, or taken alone for GLP-1/metabolic effects.

🧬 Health & Science

GLP-1 Drugs Activate Hunger Neurons to Drive Fat Oxidation (Nick Norwitz)

New research finding: GLP-1 receptor agonists turn on AgRP "hunger neurons," which seems paradoxical for a drug that suppresses appetite. When those neurons are chemically blocked in animal models, the drugs initially cause weight loss but the weight rebounds aggressively even with continued caloric restriction. The explanation: AgRP neurons have a second function — shifting metabolism from carbohydrate oxidation to fat oxidation, essentially mimicking a ketogenic metabolic state. It's this fat-burning shift, not appetite suppression alone, that produces durable fat loss. Implication: the neurological machinery of hunger regulation is inseparable from metabolic substrate selection.

Allulose: Four Human RCTs Pointing in the Same Direction (Thomas DeLauer)

Beyond DeLauer's coverage (summarized above), the mechanism worth noting separately: allulose is structurally near-identical to fructose but stimulates GLP-1 release, blocks GLUT2 glucose transporters, and promotes hepatic glucokinase translocation — three distinct pathways, none of which other sweeteners share. The 12-week body composition RCT showing fat loss at identical caloric intake (only variable: sweetener) is the most striking finding, if replicated. Current FDA classification as a "rare sugar" (not artificial sweetener) means it counts toward carbohydrate grams on labels but is largely unabsorbed.

Myostatin Inhibitor (Apitegromab) — First FDA-Approved, With Major Caveats (Renaissance Periodization)

The drug demonstrated 55% lean mass preservation versus tirzepatide alone in a 24-week dieting trial, with no reduction in fat loss. The physiological mechanism is real: myostatin is a brake on skeletal muscle growth that evolved to prevent runaway muscle hypertrophy under caloric scarcity conditions; blocking it allows near-maximal muscle protein synthesis. The practical barrier right now is delivery (hospital IV infusion only) and cost (~$350K/year). The signal from this approval is that the class works; the race is now to develop subcutaneous injectable or oral small-molecule versions, which multiple companies have in pipeline.

Why Were Victorian Elites So Effective?

Samuel Hughes in Works in Progress argues that Victorian British elites succeeded not despite their apparent vices (late rising, heavy drinking, constant socializing) but through a specific form of networked trust and accountability built through those social rituals. Their schools were academically weak and STEM-light, yet they produced outsized institutional builders — the argument being that character, loyalty networks, and comfort with responsibility were the actual outputs being cultivated. A genuinely interesting counterpoint to modern meritocratic assumptions about what elite formation should look like.

In Vienna and Beijing, the First Nuclear Clocks Begin to Tick

The first operational thorium-229 nuclear clocks have been demonstrated — clocks that use a nuclear transition (rather than an electron transition in atomic clocks) as their oscillator. They promise precision orders of magnitude beyond current atomic clocks, with implications for GPS, fundamental physics tests, and dark matter detection. A genuine scientific milestone that's been theorized for decades.

Stoicism and Wealth (Massimo Pigliucci)

Pigliucci works through the genuine tension in Stoic thought between Epictetus (who was enslaved and poor, deeply suspicious of wealth) and Seneca (fabulously wealthy, who argued money is a "preferred indifferent" — fine to have, as long as you're not enslaved to it). The resolution isn't "wealth is fine" but something more demanding: that you must be genuinely willing to lose it without your character or equanimity changing. A useful framework for anyone thinking about the relationship between financial success and what the Stoics call the "good life."
