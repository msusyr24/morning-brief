Worth your time:

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz breaks down a genuinely surprising Nature paper: clonal hematopoiesis — mutant bone marrow stem cells that expand with age, skew inflammatory, and migrate into artery walls to drive atherosclerosis — is far more common than most people realize, and may be a more important driver of heart disease than cholesterol. The key finding is that exercise and sleep act as "cellular snipers," suppressing the expansion of these specific mutant clones in the bone marrow AND reprogramming their inflammatory behavior once they reach arteries — through two distinct, complementary mechanisms. In a human RCT just published in Nature Metabolism (130 adults, 4 groups), one year of exercise produced a statistically significant 7% regression of arterial plaque on imaging; the GLP-1 drug alone group showed no significant benefit. His bottom line: exercise and sleep aren't just good habits — they're the only known free interventions that reach into your bone marrow and selectively target the cells that quietly drive your cardiovascular risk as you age.

🧬 Health & Science

Clonal Hematopoiesis: The Hidden Cardiovascular Risk Factor Exercise and Sleep Directly Target

Nick Norwitz's breakdown (see YouTube section above) is the most substantive health science item today. The core mechanism: with age, stem cells in your bone marrow accumulate mutations that give them a replication advantage. These clonal lineages are inflammatory, they expand and crowd out diverse healthy lineages, and then they migrate into artery walls and drive atherosclerosis. This is not a rare exotic condition — it becomes increasingly prevalent each decade of life and is likely present in a large fraction of older adults with no awareness. The Nature paper Norwitz covers found that exercise selectively suppressed specific inflammatory clones (not blood cell production generally) in both human observational data (90,000+ in UK Biobank and All of Us) and mouse experiments, while sleep disruption accelerated clone expansion and made the clones already in arteries more inflammatory. The human RCT confirmation (Nature Metabolism, 2026): exercise → 7% plaque regression over 1 year; GLP-1 alone → no significant effect. The practical implication is that sleep and exercise aren't just risk-reducers in a vague sense — they're operating on a specific, newly characterized molecular pathway that no drug currently targets.

Allulose: Mechanisms Behind the Body Composition Findings

Thomas DeLauer covers data worth knowing if you're thinking about sweeteners. The 121-person RCT (3 months, sucralose vs. low/high allulose at identical calories) showed the high-allulose group (7g twice daily) had significantly lower body fat percentage, BMI, and subcutaneous and abdominal fat. The proposed mechanisms go beyond "low calorie" in two directions: (1) allulose stimulates GLP-1, reducing insulin response by ~33% and slowing gastric emptying; (2) more unusually, rodent data shows allulose downregulates three key intestinal fatty acid transport proteins, meaning less dietary fat is absorbed — excess fat passes through to stool. The liver effect is separate: allulose also downregulates hepatic fatty acid synthase and storage enzymes, reducing both the synthesis and import of liver fat. The practical application he suggests: take it with higher-fat meals (eggs, fatty proteins) since that's when the fat transport reduction is most mechanistically relevant. Allulose is classified as a rare sugar, not an artificial sweetener, and is found naturally in small amounts in figs and raisins.

How to Decatastrophize the Worst-Case Scenario

Donald Robertson (author of How to Think Like a Roman Emperor) covers the Stoic practice of premeditatio malorum — deliberately imagining worst-case outcomes not to induce anxiety but to defuse the catastrophizing that makes adversity feel unbearable before it arrives. The specific technique: ask whether the feared outcome is truly as bad as it feels, whether you could cope with it, and whether your judgment of it is driven by the event itself or by your interpretation of it. A short, practical piece rather than a survey article.
