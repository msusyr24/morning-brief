Modern Wisdom – Martin Seligman

Seligman, the founder of positive psychology, traces the field's origin to three blind spots that dominated psychology 60 years ago: the exclusive focus on misery, the exclusion of thinking/cognition, and hard determinism. His key empirical finding: when exposed to inescapable adversity, 5/8 of people become helpless and passive — but 3/8 don't, and the differentiator is optimism. Pessimists explain failure as permanent, global, and internal ("I'm stupid"); optimists explain it as temporary and situational ("I had a hangover"). Crucially, he argues optimism is learnable, not just inherited: specific cognitive techniques — identifying catastrophic automatic thoughts, disputing them with evidence — measurably shift explanatory style. His garden epiphany with his 5-year-old daughter reframed parenting and therapy: the goal isn't correcting errors down to zero, it's identifying and building on strengths.

Videos: Positive Psychology Founder: How To Stop Your Negative Thoughts

GTO Lab – Mystery Bounty Strategy

Nick Petrangelo, Daniel Dvoress, and Axel Hallay walk through a new GTO Lab mystery bounty solver library — the core argument being that mystery bounty tournaments are massively +EV for studied players because almost nobody has correct preflop strategy, especially at mid-stages (100–1,000 players left). The key concept is the "KO stack ratio": bounty value divided by villain's stack size, which gives a fast in-game shortcut for how aggressively to hunt. They demonstrate that hands losing pure tournament EV (jack-ten offsuit, etc.) become clear calls once KO EV is layered in — and the tools visualize this split explicitly. Axel also built an Excel calculator for accurate bounty chip valuation that corrects the old "add chips to pot" approximation, which can be off by 2.5x. Petrangelo's honest summary: even after studying millions of hands, he had no idea how to play these spots before the library.

Videos: NOBODY KNEW HOW TO PLAY MYSTERY BOUNTIES — UNTIL NOW

Jared Alderman – Hidden Information & Exploits with Uri Peleg

Uri Peleg opens with a sharp insight: it only matters if you bluff too much or too little if your opponent calls too much or too little — meaning global frequency errors are effectively invisible unless you telegraph them. He uses a chess analogy to frame GTO: each player maintains an invisible "board" (range construction), and exploitative play is making an informed guess about your opponent's board setup. On the practical question of whether elite players have systematic tendencies: Peleg argues that decision fatigue creates exploitable patterns, and that even small asymmetries (over-bluffing ace-high boards, under-bluffing king-high boards) are undetectable without disclosure. His core advice on randomizing bet sizes: theoretically interesting, practically irrelevant unless you've fully mastered the one-size game tree first. He also makes the point that not RNGing doesn't make you unbalanced if your opponent can't detect your pattern.

Videos: "Poker Doesn't Work Without Exploits" - Uri Peleg

Peter Attia – Cancer Science with Siddhartha Mukherjee

Mukherjee updates the cancer landscape 15 years after Emperor of All Maladies: the most important shift is that immunotherapy now meaningfully extends survival in lung cancer (previously a near-death sentence), and CAR-T cell therapy produces genuine 15–20 year survivors in blood cancers. On the Vogelstein "bad luck" finding (≈66% of mutations are random): Mukherjee notes the 5% hereditary fraction is now outdated — polygenic "nudge mutations" (hundreds of small-effect variants stacking together) explain much of hereditary risk that single-gene models missed. On early detection: liquid biopsies (blood-based cancer tests) probably don't work in general populations due to false positives, but may be valuable in high-risk individuals. Colorectal cancer rising in young people remains a genuine mystery. The most exciting near-term treatment frontier is personalized cancer vaccines (neoantigen-based), with a phase III melanoma trial showing real signal.

Videos: 412 ‒ The evolving science of cancer

Thomas DeLauer – Sleep Doctor on Waking at 3AM

Dr. Bruce (clinical psychologist and sleep specialist) explains the 3AM wake mechanism: core body temperature must rise between 1–3AM to prevent hypothermia, which pushes you into lighter sleep stages and lowers the arousal threshold. The problem isn't the waking — it's that frustration about being awake spikes anxiety, heart rate, and temperature, exactly the three things you need lowered to fall back asleep. His protocol: don't get up to pee unless you truly must (roll onto your back, count to 20 first — 70% of side sleepers feel false bladder pressure); don't look at the clock. For falling back asleep, he prescribes 4-7-8 breathing (inhale 4, hold 7, exhale 8) for 20 cycles, visualizing the numbers to block ruminative thought — the extended exhale specifically activates the parasympathetic nervous system. He distinguishes this from box breathing: the key is that the exhale must be longer than the hold. He recommends a ramp: 4-5-6 for a week, then 4-6-7, then 4-7-8.

Skin Tags as Hyperinsulinemia Biomarker (Ken Berry)

Berry's claim is clinically specific enough to flag: skin tags — especially multiple or larger ones — correlate with hyperinsulinemia, and he cites a fasting insulin threshold of 13–14 µIU/mL as "severely" elevated with increased cancer, cardiovascular, and stroke risk. He argues they resolve without removal when fasting insulin drops below 10 (ideally ~5) through carbohydrate restriction. This is consistent with published literature on acanthosis nigricans and insulin resistance as shared mechanisms, though the exact thresholds he cites are debated.

Ken Berry MD

3AM Waking: Core Body Temperature as Primary Mechanism

From the DeLauer/sleep doctor conversation: the mechanistic explanation for near-universal 1–3AM waking is thermoregulation, not cortisol or stress per se. Core temperature must rise to prevent hypothermia, which shifts sleep to lighter stages. The 4-7-8 breathing protocol works via two mechanisms: the extended exhale activates parasympathetic tone, and full lung inflation (all lobes) allows slower, more efficient oxygen distribution — meaning heart rate doesn't need to increase to compensate for shallow breathing. He cites that a heart rate above 60 BPM mechanically blocks the transition to unconsciousness for most people.

Gaius Blossius, the Revolutionary Stoic

Pigliucci profiles a lesser-known Stoic who was advisor to Tiberius Gracchus and later supported an anti-Roman rebellion in Asia — making the case that Stoicism is not inherently quietist or politically passive. The argument: Stoic commitment to justice and the common good can demand action, not mere equanimity. Useful corrective to the popular misconception of Stoicism as purely inward-focused emotional regulation.

Robertson covers the Stoic technique of premeditation applied specifically to anger — rehearsing in advance the situations likely to trigger it so the emotional reaction is weaker when it arrives. A research paper investigating why readers find AI-generated text harder to comprehend than human-written text even when controlling for content and accuracy — pointing to subtle differences in sentence structure, cohesion, and information density that don't register as "wrong" but impede understanding.
