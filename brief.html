Jared Alderman

Jared makes a point that cuts through a lot of study-room confusion: the right way to think in a hand isn't "what's my solver output?" but rather asking two questions in order — what is their range, and what happens if I bet/check/raise? He argues that most players, even serious ones, aren't actually "in the hand" — they're floating in an abstract mental model of poker they've constructed from solver study, disconnected from what's actually in front of them. He notes that getting concrete about your opponent's actual holdings plants you in reality and that most spots reduce to just three situations: value betting, bluffing, or bluff catching. The GTO Lab clip reinforces the same idea from a live/behavioral angle: the one thing you fully control at the table is how you carry yourself — no strategy talk, no reaction to bad beats — and that composure is itself an edge that compounds over time.

Nick Norwitz

Nick shares the backstory that shaped his entire public work: he completed his MD/PhD at Harvard/Oxford while managing severe ulcerative colitis that conventional medicine couldn't resolve, at one point considering suicide as preferable to the ongoing suffering. A ketogenic diet put his IBD into remission when nothing else had worked — not because he expected it to, but out of desperation. The experience forced him to confront a contradiction he couldn't un-see: he was trained in evidence-based medicine, yet the intervention that saved his life had no RCT for IBD. He found this story was far from unique — countless patients with chronic, autoimmune, and metabolic conditions were quietly resolving "incurable" conditions with dietary changes their doctors never mentioned. His conclusion: the most powerful thing medicine can offer isn't a prescription or a surgery, but curiosity and the empowerment to treat your own body as an experiment. This is a genuinely good piece of personal narrative that explains his intellectual position, not just his brand.

Peter Attia (The Drive)

Attia interviews Michael (a cardiologist/lipidologist at U Chicago) on the case for early, aggressive LDL intervention — starting in your 30s or even younger. The core argument is the "8 gram rule": roughly 8 grams of cumulative lifetime LDL-cholesterol exposure triggers atherosclerotic disease, whether that's 200 mg/dL × 40 years or 100 mg/dL × 80 years. Keeping LDL under ~80 mg/dL lifelong may prevent cardiovascular disease entirely. The guest's own story is a compelling n=1: he started statins in medical school after his father died at 47; his brother, same lipid genetics, went vegetarian and avoided statins until age 44, then needed bypass surgery. The key reframe for why to act young: LDL is a causal driver of ASCVD (established by Mendelian randomization + clinical trials), so the logic is the same as for smoking cessation — you don't wait for 15 pack-years of damage before acting. They also touch on CETPi drugs (obicetrapib/New Amsterdam Pharma) as a potentially new LDL-lowering mechanism class and the frustrating reality that primary prevention guidelines still don't reflect this early-intervention thinking.

Andrew Huberman / Andy Galpin

Galpin lays out a nine-adaptation framework for physical fitness — skill, speed, power, strength, hypertrophy, muscular endurance, anaerobic capacity, aerobic capacity (VO2 max), and long-duration endurance — and gives cheap self-assessment tests for each. Key benchmarks: broad jump = your own body height (power); grip hang = 30–60+ seconds (strength/longevity); hand grip dynamometer ≥ 40 kg men / 35 kg women; no more than 10% L/R grip asymmetry. For movement screening, he recommends filming yourself from front and side doing push-up, pull-up, squat, and deadlift, checking for symmetry, stability, joint awareness, and full range of motion. The broader point is that most people are undertrained in at least one of these nine categories and have no idea which one, which makes this a useful diagnostic rather than just a fitness list.

🧬 Health & Science

The Case for Treating LDL in Your 30s (Peter Attia / Michael)

The "8 gram rule" is a useful concrete framework: atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease appears to be triggered by roughly 8 grams of cumulative lifetime LDL-cholesterol exposure (LDL in mg/dL × years). This means 200 mg/dL for 40 years = same risk as 100 mg/dL for 80 years. Keeping LDL below 80 mg/dL lifelong may prevent ASCVD almost entirely — a claim grounded in Mendelian randomization data showing lifetime low-LDL genotypes are highly cardioprotective. The guest's n=1 comparison (himself vs. his brother, same genetics, different statin timing, dramatically different cardiac outcomes) is anecdote but striking. The implication for a 30-something with elevated LDL: 10-year risk calculators are the wrong frame; causal risk factors should be treated regardless of time horizon, exactly as we treat smoking or hypertension. Current ACC/AHA guidelines lag significantly on this.

Ketogenic Diet for Epilepsy: The Clinical Case (Anthony Chaffee)

Chaffee makes a point worth filing: ketogenic diet as epilepsy treatment has over a century of clinical literature and remains a first-line intervention at Johns Hopkins. His practical protocol for someone wanting to trial it alongside existing medication — drop carbs below 20g/day for adults (10g for children), increase dietary fat to compensate, monitor blood ketones (not urine), stay on medications until seizure-free for 3–6 months, then taper only under physician supervision. The counterintuitive point he addresses: stopping keto doesn't cause an immediate seizure rebound the way stopping anti-epileptic drugs does — seizure-free duration is itself protective regardless of intervention type.
