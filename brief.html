Jared Alderman / GTO Lab

Jared and Uri Peleg dig into a genuinely underexplored angle: hidden information in poker strategy. Uri's core point is that exploitative deviations only matter if your opponent can detect them — if you over-bluff ace-high boards and under-bluff king-high boards in a globally balanced way, there's no practical mechanism for anyone to discover that asymmetry. He also makes a sharp distinction between GTO strategy and execution: even elite players haven't fully mapped the game tree at a one-size level, so multi-sizing randomization is theoretically interesting but practically irrelevant for almost everyone. Separately, the GTO Lab crew (Nick Petrangelo, Daniel Dvoress, and Axel Hallay) break down mystery bounty tournaments as a genuinely different game — not "play a little looser" but sometimes VPIP 100% from early position, because at high KO-to-stack ratios the bounty EV is entirely yellow (pure KO value, zero prize pool contribution). The KO-to-stack ratio is the golden ratio driving all pre/post-flop adjustments; GTO Lab has built ~300 sims covering this.

Lex Fridman

An emergency panel of three mathematicians (fluid dynamics, complexity theory, number theory) responds to OpenAI apparently solving Navier-Stokes, a quasi-Riemann Hypothesis result, the Unique Games Conjecture, and hundreds of other results. The core debate: are the AI proofs inside the "convex hull" of existing mathematical ideas (recombining known moves very well) or genuinely expanding the boundary? The consensus leans toward a "semigroup" framing — not derogatory linear combination, but a space of known moves whose reachable area is far larger than any human had explored. The sharpest concern isn't about proof-finding per se; it's the "doorman fallacy" — reducing math to theorem-proving and then declaring it automated, while ignoring what mathematicians actually do: asking the right questions, building new conceptual structures, and creating theories that make surrounding problems fall. Terence Tao's alarm about PhD students losing motivating problems is taken seriously; the panel notes there's no putting the genie back once a solution exists.

Thomas DeLauer

Two substantive videos. On supplement timing: Vitamin D3 should be taken 6–10am (aligns with cortisol rise and natural UV synthesis window), but never without K2 — K2 activates osteocalcin, the "air traffic controller" that directs calcium into bone rather than arteries and soft tissue. Collagen is best taken at night (5–10g before bed) because collagen synthesis peaks during early deep sleep alongside growth hormone, meaning you stack the external peptide signal on top of peak internal demand; morning dosing is fine for tendon tensile effects but misses this window. For magnesium: glycinate/threonate at night (crosses blood-brain barrier, builds serotonin/melatonin, cited a 7,500-person study tying magnesium to sleep quality); malate or di-magnesium malate during the day for sustained energy support without sedation. On mustard for insulin resistance: the active compound AITC (allyl isothiocyanate, from mustard seeds — requires stone-ground or Dijon, not yellow mustard) increased GLUT2 transporters in a rodent model, improved pancreatic beta-cell signaling, raised antioxidant capacity, and reduced pro-inflammatory gene expression via NRF2 upregulation. It activates TRPA1 receptors — the same pungency/irritant pathway that creates dietary-induced thermogenesis — and significantly curbs appetite through what the cited study called "sensory satisfaction."

🧬 Health & Science

Brown Fat: Large-Scale Evidence and Activation Methods

Nick Norwitz flagged a Nature study (100,000+ participants) finding that individuals with detectable brown fat had 22–48% lower odds of diabetes, heart disease, depression, and anxiety. Brown fat is mitochondria-dense and burns calories rather than storing them. The study identified losartan (an ARB blood pressure medication) as one activator; cold exposure, exercise, and at least one dietary strategy (Norwitz claims 600 kcal/day increase in energy expenditure in RCTs — not named in the short, requires the linked masterclass) are others. The paradox finding — higher brown fat associated with higher LDL — is worth sitting with given the conventional LDL-as-villain framework.

Cold Immersion Mechanistics: After Drop, Shivering, and Insulin Sensitivity

Dr. Susanna Søberg (via Huberman) clarified the "after drop" mechanism: upon exiting cold water, previously constricted blood vessels dilate, sending now-cooled peripheral blood back to the core, dropping core temperature — this is what triggers shivering, not the water itself. Shivering releases succinate, which activates brown fat, making the post-cold shiver a feature not a bug. A 2016 study she cited found one winter swimming season (4–5 months) lowered blood pressure, heart rate, and improved insulin sensitivity in middle-aged adults. Her protocol recommendation: end sessions on cold rather than warming (sauna then cold, not cold then sauna) to force this hormetic training response.

Supplement Timing via Chronobiology (DeLauer)

Thomas DeLauer made a mechanistically grounded case for timing specificity: (1) D3 without K2 pulls calcium into the bloodstream with no routing signal — K2 activates osteocalcin to direct calcium to bone vs. arteries; always pair them and take in the morning with fat. (2) Collagen peptides are signaling molecules, not raw material delivery — they trigger endogenous collagen production, which peaks alongside growth hormone in early deep sleep, so evening dosing (5–10g, 1 hour before bed) stacks the external signal on the internal peak. (3) Magnesium form matters by time of day: glycinate/threonate at night (sedating, crosses BBB, supports melatonin); malate/di-magnesium malate during the day (sustained release, supports muscle relaxation without drowsiness). These aren't vague biohacking claims — the mechanisms are specific and testable.
