Feynman Technique

Jared Alderman

Alderman's core coaching thesis: most players play from a fear-based mental model — asking "what's the worst that can happen?" instead of "what should theoretically be happening here, and what is actually happening?" The fix is building intuition through data, not trying to suppress fear directly. He gives a sharp PLO example: flopping the nut straight with no backup (e.g., 89 no flush draw on J-T-7) actually requires a check-back at high frequency, but most players pot it immediately out of fear of getting sucked out — which is precisely backwards. The solver says bet less because your nut probability by the river is low; top set bets more because it retains its value on board pairs and can stack second- and third-best hands. He also touches on meditation, arguing that what most people call "presence" is actually attachment to positive affect — when the good feeling disappears, they think they've "lost presence," but every moment is equally available for it.

🧬 Health & Science

Fasting Insulin as Early Diabetes Predictor (Ken Berry MD)

The standard diabetes screening (fasting glucose, A1C) catches prediabetes 2–3 years before diagnosis. Fasting insulin above 15 µIU/mL can identify insulin resistance a decade or more before type 2 diabetes develops — giving a much larger intervention window. Most physicians don't order it. This aligns with Sten Ekberg's parallel point: a patient can have an A1C of 5.6, glucose of 95 (both below flagging thresholds), yet have a HOMA-IR of 2.0 with significant arterial and lipid dysfunction already underway. The actionable takeaway: request a fasting insulin test at your next physical.

Pulse Pressure as Vascular Health Marker (Dr. Sten Ekberg)

Pulse pressure (systolic minus diastolic) should ideally be 30–40 mmHg. A reading above 50 indicates arterial stiffness — the aorta's elastin is degrading, reducing its ability to sustain blood flow between heartbeats and cutting coronary artery perfusion (which is fed during the diastolic rebound). Causes of elastin degradation: glycation from chronic high blood glucose (insulin resistance), inflammation, and simple mechanical wear over ~3 billion heartbeats. The 2025 blood pressure guidelines doubled down on medicating the raw numbers (target: 130/80) without addressing pulse pressure at all, meaning medication may reduce systolic/diastolic readings without fixing the underlying arterial stiffness driving heart failure and stroke risk.

Monk Fruit Mogrosides: 5-RCT Meta-Analysis on Glucose and Inflammation (Thomas DeLauer)

A PRISMA-guided systematic review in Nutrients pooling 5 randomized controlled trials found monk fruit extract (mogrosides) reduced postprandial glucose by 10–18% and insulin response by up to 22%. One RCT showed a 25% reduction in IL-6 and TNF-alpha — the two most well-characterized inflammatory cytokines — at a small dose. Mechanistically, mogrosides are triterpenoid glycosides unrecognizable to salivary and intestinal enzymes as carbohydrates (glycemic index: zero) and appear to downregulate NF-κB gene expression, which is the master regulator of inflammatory signaling. Longer-term data: 23% reduction in "sugar reinforcement behavior" with regular use compared to sucrose — meaning it may actually reduce cravings rather than sustain them (contrary to conventional wisdom about sweet-tasting non-nutritive sweeteners). Caveat: one study found that monk fruit beverages led to higher food intake at the next meal, suggesting appetite may rebound acutely.

Exercise Intensity and Cardiometabolic Health (Cell Reports Medicine)

A new study in Cell Reports Medicine (84 pts on HN) examines the association between exercise intensity and cardiometabolic health markers. While the full paper requires reading, this is a Cell-family journal publication on a core longevity question — exercise dose-response for metabolic outcomes — worth tracking against Peter Attia's consistent framing that both intensity (VO2 max improvement) and volume matter independently.

Stoicism versus Daoism — Donald Robertson

Robertson compares Stoic and Daoist philosophy directly — both traditions counsel acceptance of what cannot be controlled, but they differ sharply on the role of reason and virtue: Stoics hold that virtue (rational excellence) is the only true good and must be actively pursued; Daoists counsel non-striving (wu wei) and alignment with natural flow rather than rational will. Worth reading if you've been sitting with Marcus Aurelius — the contrast sharpens what's distinctive about the Stoic commitment to rational agency versus Eastern acceptance traditions.
