Peter Attia (The Drive)

Attia sits with cardiologist/lipidologist Michael (CEO of New Amsterdam Pharmaceuticals, runs the lipid clinic at U Chicago) for a deep dive on primordial prevention. The core argument: the mainstream model — wait for significant plaque or a cardiac event, then treat aggressively — is backwards. The "8 gram rule" is the memorable heuristic: 8 grams of cumulative lifetime LDL exposure (200 mg/dL × 40 years = 8g; or 80 mg/dL × 100 years = 8g) is the threshold associated with heart disease. Michael's personal n=1: he started statins in med school after his father died at 47; his brother (also a doctor, went vegetarian instead, no early statin) had bypass surgery at 44 — same genetics, decade of difference in treatment.

🧬 Health & Science

Primordial LDL Prevention: The 8-Gram Rule (Peter Attia / The Drive)

The most clinically actionable framing from this week: cumulative lifetime LDL exposure, not 10-year risk calculators, is the right frame for prevention. The "8 gram rule" (LDL-C in mg/dL × years of exposure = lifetime burden) suggests that keeping LDL below ~80 mg/dL throughout your life prevents atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. The smoking cessation analogy is rhetorically powerful and mechanistically correct — LDL is causal via Mendelian randomization studies, so there's no defensible reason to wait for plaque before treating it, just as no doctor should tell a new smoker to keep going for another decade. The key practical point: the 30s are arguably the most important decade because that's when plaque formation accelerates, not the 60s when most guidelines kick in.

Ketogenic Diet and Alzheimer's: The Ketone Metabolism Hypothesis (Anthony Chaffee)

The mechanistic thread in the Dean & Jody episode worth understanding: Alzheimer's is increasingly framed as "type 3 diabetes" — a state where neurons can no longer efficiently use glucose as fuel. Mary Newport's work with MCT oil in her Alzheimer's-diagnosed husband showed meaningful cognitive improvement (clock-drawing ability returned within days) because MCT oil provides ketones as an alternative fuel source the brain can still metabolize. The carnivore/ketogenic intervention in Jody's case is built on this mechanism — deprive the brain of glucose, provide ketones instead, and reduce the inflammatory load of processed carbohydrates simultaneously. Not RCT-level evidence, but the mechanistic plausibility is real and the Bredesen protocol literature provides some backing.

Sesame Seeds and the Estrogen Metabolite Ratio (Thomas DeLauer)

Specific mechanism worth knowing: sesame lignans (sesamin and sesamolin) inhibit CYP3A4, the enzyme that drives production of 16-alpha-hydroxyestrone — the "bad" proliferative estrogen metabolite. A human crossover study in post-menopausal women (50g sesame powder daily × 5 weeks) found the 2-hydroxy:16-alpha ratio improved by 47%, with the protective metabolite up 72%. Separate rodent data showed dose-dependent testosterone and Leydig cell increases. The practical upshot: sesame/tahini (2–4 tbsp ground, daily) attacks the estrogen-side drag on testosterone through a mechanism entirely distinct from tongkat ali, and stacks logically with apigenin (which inhibits aromatase upstream). The SERM-like behavior — agonist at low estrogen, partial antagonist at high — is the most pharmacologically interesting detail.

The Psychiatric Crisis: No DSM-Based Breakthrough After $20B (Lex Fridman / Andrew Scull)

Historian of psychiatry Andrew Scull makes a damning case: Thomas Insel, after 13 years leading NIMH and overseeing $20 billion in neuroscience/genetics research, admitted on leaving that "the lot of the mentally ill has improved not one bit." The DSM-5 project was designed to replace symptom-based diagnosis with pathology-based diagnosis (brain markers, genetics) and failed — reverting to the 1980 DSM-III symptomatic framework. The core problem Scull identifies: diagnostic reliability (two clinicians reaching the same conclusion) was achieved, but validity (whether the categories reflect biological reality) was not. The distinction between schizophrenia as a unified category vs. "the schizophrenias" (Bleuler's original framing) is the diagnostic knife that may eventually cut through. Worth reading/listening for a sober map of where psychiatry actually stands.
