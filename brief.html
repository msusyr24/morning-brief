🎥 From People I Follow

Worth your time:

Anthony Chaffee MD

Chaffee makes the case that crooked teeth and underdeveloped jaws are purely nutritional in origin — not genetic — pointing to pre-agricultural skulls that universally show wide jaws and fully erupted wisdom teeth. His specific mechanism: Vitamin K2 is fat-soluble and animal-derived; K1 from plants converts to K2 at roughly 1%, meaning plant-heavy diets leave people chronically K2-deficient. K2 works in concert with calcium and D3 to drive proper jaw and dental development, and without adequate mechanical stimulus from chewing dense animal foods, jaw growth is further impaired. Short clip, but a clean summary of the carnivore/K2 thesis applied to a visible, measurable outcome.

🧬 Health & Science

Is the Correlation Between Hypertrophy and Strength Gains Stronger Than We Realized?

Stronger by Science reviews new data suggesting muscle hypertrophy contributes more to strength gains in novice lifters than prior studies indicated — prior work may have underestimated this relationship due to measurement insensitivity. Critically, the authors walk back any overinterpretation: the correlation is still far from deterministic, meaning hypertrophy and strength remain meaningfully dissociable outcomes even early in training. Relevant for programming decisions around whether hypertrophy-focused training translates to strength for beginners.

Bioengineered chewing gum may offer a way to fight HPV and other microbes

Researchers developed a chewing gum embedded with plant-derived proteins that bind and neutralize HPV viral particles in saliva, reducing oral viral load in preliminary models. The mechanism is passive delivery of neutralizing agents through normal chewing behavior — no injection, no systemic drug. Early stage, but an interesting example of mucosal delivery as a therapeutic route.

Mario Meets Pareto

An unusually well-crafted interactive essay that uses Mario level design to explain the Pareto front — the set of solutions where you can't improve one objective without sacrificing another. Goes beyond the standard 80/20 framing and into actual multi-objective optimization logic, which is a genuinely useful mental model for any domain involving tradeoffs (training load vs. recovery, risk vs. return, etc.). High-upvote HN consensus that this is one of the better explainers of the concept.

Taste Is All That's Left

The essay argues that as AI commoditizes execution — writing, coding, designing — the scarce and durable skill becomes taste: the capacity to judge what's worth making, what's good, and why. It's a direct challenge to the idea that prompt engineering or tool fluency is the meaningful edge; the author's claim is that aesthetic and critical judgment are the last moat. Worth sitting with given how often the opposite gets assumed.

Humans missed 1 in 3 threats approving AI agent commands across 40k game runs

A study across 40,000 simulated runs found that human overseers failed to catch roughly 33% of dangerous or adversarial AI agent commands when acting as approval gatekeepers. The finding directly undermines the "human in the loop" assumption that underwrites most current AI deployment safety arguments — if humans rubber-stamp a third of bad actions at scale, oversight is largely theatrical. Directly relevant to anyone thinking about AI reliability and real-world trust calibration.

The River and the Labyrinth – Parts I & II (Pigliucci)

Massimo Pigliucci's two-part series examines process philosophy — the view that reality is fundamentally constituted by change and events rather than static substances — starting with Whitehead and then pulling back to Heraclitus as the more honest and tractable starting point. Part II argues Whitehead's metaphysics becomes so technically elaborate it loses contact with lived experience, while Heraclitus' river metaphor captures the essential insight more cleanly: identity persists through flux, not despite it. Useful framing for anyone wrestling with Stoic ideas about impermanence and the nature of the self.

Figs in Winter · Part II

Be Mindful Not to Injure Your Own Character — Commentary on Epictetus #38

Donald Robertson's ongoing Handbook of Epictetus commentary reaches section 38, which concerns how social pressures — laughter, flattery, conformity — erode character through small capitulations. Epictetus' argument is that protecting your own rational faculty and moral character is the one thing actually in your control, and that every compromise to external approval is a real cost, not a neutral social lubricant. Robertson ties this to Stoic practice around self-monitoring and the inner citadel. Compact and worth the five minutes.
