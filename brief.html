🎥 From People I Follow

Worth your time:

Nick Norwitz

Norwitz makes a strong mechanistic case for the vagus nerve as a literal anti-inflammatory dial. The core finding: stressing mice with severe IBD knocked their vagal tone down ~3x, and the entire stress group died within days — but surgically stimulating the vagus nerve reversed the outcome, dropping inflammatory markers (IL-6, TNF-alpha) ~70% via a process called SUMOylation. In humans, a 242-person randomized double-blind trial (RESET-R) showed vagus nerve stimulation significantly improved rheumatoid arthritis in patients who had already failed multiple drugs — and slowed measurable bone erosion on MRI. Practical free lever: "cyclic sighing" (double inhale through nose, long slow exhale through mouth), which a Stanford RCT found outperformed mindfulness meditation for mood and physiological arousal. Norwitz frames this as directly relevant to anyone with chronic stress, not just IBD patients.

Video: How to Use Your Vagus Nerve to Improve Gut Health

Thomas DeLauer

DeLauer draws a clean distinction between two separate walking-timing questions that get conflated online. For blood sugar: post-meal walking wins clearly — a 2023 meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found walking within 30 minutes after eating beat pre-meal walking for glycemic control, because muscle contraction pulls glucose from circulation independently of insulin. For visceral/liver fat: pre-meal fasted walking wins, because it depletes liver glycogen first, so incoming carbs refill glycogen rather than spilling into de novo lipogenesis (fat synthesis). His protocol: sandwich the meal — 10 minutes before + 10-20 minutes after — to hit both targets. Separately, he digs into the avocado oil adulteration story with the president of the Avocado Oil Manufacturers Association, who confirms adulteration likely occurs but that Codex standards only became active in late 2024 (vs. 44 years of olive oil standards), so there's no regulatory "smoking gun" yet — bad actors can hide in the definitional gap.

Videos: The Best Time to Walk to Stop Visceral Fat is NOT After a Meal · The Avocado Oil Controversy

Renaissance Periodization (Dr. Mike)

Dr. Mike got a full-body MRI after 15 years of PED use and shares the results transparently. Headline findings: no enlarged organs detected (he was genuinely surprised), no obvious cancerous masses, and less visceral fat than the average 42-year-old American — but with a striking inversion pattern the radiologist said he'd never seen: almost zero subcutaneous fat but relatively high intra-abdominal/posterior visceral fat. The doctor's conclusion: Dr. Mike is "thin on the outside, fat on the inside" at the highest ratio ever seen. His explanation for his persistent gut: loose abdominal wall (genetic, confirmed by asymmetry in his rectus abdominis), not visceral fat or organ size. Practical takeaways he pulls: regular bloodwork is non-negotiable for enhanced athletes, tirzepatide preferentially burns visceral fat, and the fat cell count problem (cells shrink but don't disappear after a dreamer bulk) means his VAT will likely return when he masses up. Peter Attia

Attia's thyroid AMA covers why the field is so contentious: thyroid follows a Goldilocks curve (too much and too little both cause serious harm), the main secreted hormone (T4) is a pro-hormone that converts locally tissue-by-tissue to active T3, and TSH — the standard lab marker — is a step removed from what's actually happening in the liver, heart, or brain. He identifies two failure modes: (1) dismissing a real problem because TSH is "normal" at 4.2 despite positive antibodies and family history, and (2) the functional medicine overcorrection that treats borderline reverse-T3 as irrefutable tissue-level hypothyroidism and builds an elaborate root-cause narrative that isn't supported. His key empirical point: a large share of mildly elevated TSH readings will normalize spontaneously on repeat testing, and treating unnecessarily with thyroid hormone can cause cognitive symptoms, atrial fibrillation, and bone loss.

Video: Thyroid health: interpreting symptoms, diagnosing & treating dysfunction

Jared Alderman

He reports substantial progress over years, crediting incremental exposure and the Seeking Balance app he built for poker players.

🧬 Health & Science

Semaglutide and Aging — Peter Attia

A new mouse study found semaglutide extended median lifespan and improved several aging biomarkers in female mice. Attia flags that the headline requires careful interpretation — mouse longevity data doesn't translate cleanly to humans, and the study was female-only — but notes it adds to a growing body of evidence that GLP-1 agonists may have effects beyond metabolic control.

Can semaglutide slow aging? — Peter Attia MD

Saturated Fat and Stroke — Anthony Chaffee

Chaffee cites an umbrella review (meta-analysis of meta-analyses of RCTs) that found no correlation between saturated fat intake and cardiovascular disease, and specifically found a protective association between saturated fat consumption and stroke risk — i.e., higher saturated fat intake correlated with fewer strokes. This is the highest tier of the evidence hierarchy and directly challenges the standard lipid-heart hypothesis as applied to dietary fat.

Steaks Cause STROKES and Heart Disease? Think again! — Anthony Chaffee MD

Honey vs. Processed Sugar — Paul Saladino (brief claim, low evidence weight)

Saladino's short-form claim: 30g of honey improves gut microbiome health while 30g of processed sugar damages it, and whole-food sugar (e.g., in mango) strengthens blood vessels. Worth noting as a hypothesis but the clip provides no citations — treat as directionally interesting, not settled.

Attention is all you have

A high-scoring (774 pts) essay arguing that the transformer architecture's core insight — the attention mechanism — is not just an engineering trick but a fundamental reframing of what computation can look like. Worth reading as a conceptual piece even if you're not technical; it's the kind of "why this matters philosophically" framing that's rare in AI writing.

Hacker News discussion

Spymarks, Not Watermarks

This piece distinguishes between watermarks (visible/detectable marks to prove authenticity) and "spymarks" — hidden identifiers embedded in content to track who leaked or shared it, without the target's knowledge. The privacy implications are significant: documents, images, and possibly AI-generated content could be silently tagged to trace leaks back to specific recipients. Worth understanding as this technique becomes more common in corporate and government contexts.

Hacker News discussion (380 pts)

I don't want to read what you didn't write

A pointed essay about the erosion of authentic written voice when AI drafts are submitted as personal communication. The author's argument is simple but sharp: when someone uses AI to write something they didn't actually think, the reader is being deceived about who they're in relationship with. Connects to broader questions about what communication is actually for.
