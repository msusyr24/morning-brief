Mental Model · Physics/Economics

Equilibrium

Systems settle into balances where forces cancel. Markets find price equilibrium; ecosystems find predator-prey balance; workplaces find culture equilibrium. Shifting equilibrium requires changing underlying forces, not fighting symptoms. Munger applies this to regulation: treat symptoms and you'll return to equilibrium; change incentives and the new equilibrium shifts.

🎥 From People I Follow

Worth your time:

GTO Wizard

Sharp exploit video on PLO: the core argument is that loose-passive players don't meaningfully differentiate between small and large bet sizes — they decide based on hand strength and draw potential, not pot odds. Therefore, sizing down against them (common instinct when you have a strong hand) is a mistake that costs you significant EV. The solver confirms this: when you node-lock the big blind to play like a loose-passive, the IP strategy shifts from frequent half-pot bets to mostly large sizing, and the optimal betting frequency actually drops (fewer weak bluffs, more polar value). The practical upshot: bet your strong hands large on flop AND turn against these players, and on tough rivers where you rarely get called, target the infrequent strong hands with a big sizing rather than sizing down to chase calls from air.

Jared Alderman

A live NLH hand dissection with a genuinely useful conceptual point: live players systematically undersize their deviations from GTO — they don't know how big a mistake they're making, or how big the exploit against them should be. The specific hand (A8s, flop top two on AK3, turn brings 8 for two-pair, faces a min-raise from a tight reg) illustrates how even when a player correctly reads "he probably has a set," folding is still wrong because the price is good and boats are live. The broader heuristic: when a player's mistake is calling too much, cut your weak bluffs and build pots hard with value — don't size down. The conversation also lands on why live players rationalize not studying by claiming read-based exploits, which Jared largely dismisses.

Thomas DeLauer

A large British Journal of Sports Medicine meta-analysis (15,000+ participants, 270+ RCTs) ranked exercise types by blood pressure reduction — isometric exercise, specifically wall sits, came out on top. Wall sits reduced systolic BP by ~11 mmHg and diastolic by ~5 mmHg, roughly double the effect of HIIT (~4 mmHg) and aerobic exercise (~5 mmHg). The mechanism: sustained muscle contraction compresses blood vessels, then the release drives nitric oxide production and vascular adaptation over time. A separate clinical hypertension study found a 12.9 mmHg systolic drop after just one week of wall squats. The secondary benefit is insulin-independent glucose disposal: sustained isometric contraction activates AMPK, which triggers GLUT4 translocation to cell membranes, pulling glucose into muscle without requiring insulin — making a 2-minute wall sit 15-30 minutes post-meal a meaningful intervention for insulin resistance. Practical protocol: work up to 2-minute holds, 2-3x/week until you hit the target, then drop to once weekly for maintenance.

Nick Norwitz

Cites a Stanford RCT comparing high-fiber vs. high-fermented-food diets for gut health. Fermented foods won: they produced more consistent reductions in inflammatory markers and greater microbiome diversity, while fiber responses were heterogeneous — some people saw inflammation decrease, others saw it increase. Practical starting point: one serving of something like sauerkraut daily, gradually increasing to six servings. Other options: kefir, kimchi, yogurt, natto.

Cell Identity Loss as a Driver of Human Aging — Eric Topol

Two new papers covered by Eric Topol (Substack) argue that aging is partly driven by loss of cell identity — cells drifting from their specialized states, becoming less distinctly "what they are." This is a mechanistic claim distinct from the standard telomere/oxidative stress narrative, and points toward epigenetic reprogramming as a potential intervention target. Worth reading if you follow longevity science seriously.

Reading Greek, Missing the Point (Parts I & II) — Massimo Pigliucci

Pigliucci (Figs in Winter) argues that classicists like Mary Beard and Emily Wilson, however brilliant as translators and scholars, fundamentally misread Stoicism because they approach it as a historical artifact rather than a living philosophy. The claim: knowing the Greek text is not the same as knowing the philosophy — you can read every word of the Meditations and still miss what Marcus was actually doing. Two-part series worth reading if you take Stoicism seriously as practice rather than as intellectual history.
