🎥 From People I Follow

Worth your time:

GTO Wizard

Tom Boss 21 makes a rigorous empirical case for late registration in MTTs that goes well beyond the usual ICM theory. The core finding: late-entering with ~50% of the field remaining gives a stack roughly 10–15% more ICM value than one purchased at the start — because your probability of finishing in paid positions increases while your first-place equity stays constant. Crucially, he shows this isn't just theory: Triton data (8,000 bullets, 2025–26) confirms late entrants cash at ~19% vs. ~15% for day-one players, and controlling for player skill, people outperform their own earlier bullets when re-entering late. Perhaps most counterintuitively, variance does not increase for late entrants — meaning the risk-adjusted return actually improves, implying you need a smaller bankroll to shot-take big tournaments via late reg. His practical corollary: after firing four bullets in one tournament, do not fire the fifth — the data shows bullet five-plus players crater at roughly –40% ROI, likely pure tilt.

Peter Attia

Attia interviews psychiatrist Linus Abrams (35 years in practice) on a thesis that runs against the mainstream: psychiatry optimizes for symptom reduction, not restoration of full human experience — and patients feel this gap even when medications objectively work, which is why adherence is so hard even in successful cases. Abrams makes a sharp mechanistic point about SSRIs: raising serotonin tends to suppress dopamine and norepinephrine (via receptor downregulation), which explains why patients on SSRIs often report feeling "better but blunted" — less anxious or depressed, but also less vital, motivated, or sexually engaged. He argues this is why SSRIs are arguably more effective for anxiety than depression, and that the "antidepressant" label is a marketing artifact. The second half pivots toward the role of hormones (especially estradiol) in psychiatric symptoms — a lens almost no psychiatrists are trained in — as the new direction of his practice.

🧬 Health & Science

Peter Attia / Linus Abrams: The SSRI Dopamine Suppression Mechanism

The specific mechanistic claim worth noting from the Attia-Abrams episode: SSRIs raise serotonin, but serotonin and dopamine compete for the same monoamine substrate and there is receptor cross-regulation — so net dopamine and norepinephrine often fall as serotonin rises. This creates the clinical paradox where a patient has objective symptom reduction but subjectively feels "blunted" — reduced libido, reduced motivation, less vitality. Abrams argues this is not a side effect bug but an inherent feature of serotonin-selective targeting, and is part of why even successful pharmacologic outcomes leave patients ambivalent about continuing treatment. His proposed lens: psychiatry should be tracking restoration of full function (hormonal, motivational, relational), not just symptom scores.

Avocado Oil Adulteration: UC Davis Data

Paul Saladino cites UC Davis testing finding 89% of retail avocado oils — including major brands like Chosen Foods, Primal Kitchen, and Siete — were adulterated with seed oils (sunflower, safflower). This replicates 2020 and 2023 studies finding 70–80% adulteration rates. The practical upshot: avocado oil currently cannot be trusted as a seed-oil alternative at retail; butter, tallow, ghee, and verified extra-virgin olive oil are the more reliable options until regulatory standards tighten.

Peter Attia: Multifactorial Trials — What They Can and Can't Prove

Attia's written piece on multifactorial clinical trials (the "kitchen sink" approach — intervening on diet, exercise, sleep, and medication simultaneously) makes a methodological point with real implications for how you read longevity research: when a trial bundles multiple interventions, it can tell you the package works but cannot isolate which component drove the effect or by how much. This matters when reading studies like PREDIMED or the FINGER trial — the headline result may be real, but the attribution to any single behavior (say, olive oil vs. exercise) is not established by the trial design.

Forget the Good Life, Try the Good Day — Massimo Pigliucci

Pigliucci argues that the Stoic unit of moral analysis isn't a lifetime or a career but a single day — and that this reframe is practically powerful: the good life is just a long series of good days, meaning virtue and right action are always immediately available, not deferred to some future state. This is a useful corrective to the kind of long-horizon goal-setting that leaves people perpetually preparing to live rather than actually living. Clean, well-argued, worth 10 minutes.

The Stoic Taxonomy of Anger — Donald Robertson

Robertson breaks down how the Stoics classified anger — distinguishing the initial involuntary "proto-passion" (the flinch, the spike) from the full emotion that requires our assent to a judgment ("this person wronged me and I should retaliate"). The practical implication: you are not responsible for the first movement, only for whether you endorse and amplify it. Pairs well with the Adolphs/Huberman neuroscience framing of emotions as functional states you can observe before acting on.
